Module 6: Mixed and multiple charts

When a task gives two or three visuals, you write one report about how they relate to each other. The Band 7 skill is to treat the visuals as a single story, and not as two separate descriptions glued together.

A. Understanding the question

What it shows. Two or three visuals about the same topic. Mixed means different types (a bar chart and a line graph, a table and a pie chart). Multiple means the same type repeated (three pie charts, two line graphs). Usually one visual gives the main quantity and the other gives context: a second measure, a second group or a second date.

How to recognise it. Two or more pictures, each with its own title and unit, and one task statement that mentions both: The bar chart shows … and the line graph shows ….

What to look for. What each visual measures and in what unit; the period, place or group that links them; the main feature of each; where they agree; and where they disagree. A disagreement is almost always a key feature.

What the task asks. The same as for a single chart: select and report the main features, and make comparisons where relevant. In a mixed task the comparison includes between the visuals.

Common wordings.

Wording What it means for your answer
“The bar chart shows … and the line graph shows …” Two different measures: describe each, then link them
“The charts below show … in three countries.” Multiple charts: compare the countries slice by slice or line by line
“The table shows …, and the pie chart shows …” One gives amounts, one gives shares: use each for what it does best
“The graphs show … in 1990 and 2020.” A dynamic task spread over two visuals: treat the two dates as one trend

Common combinations.

Combination Typical link How to organise it
Bar chart + line graph The same months or years, two different measures By period, with both measures in every paragraph
Table + pie chart The same population: amounts in the table, shares in the pie Overview from the pie, with figures from the table
Two line graphs The same period, two groups or two measures Compare the trends side by side
Three pie charts Three dates or three places Treat as one dynamic task, comparing each slice across the pies
Bar chart + table The same categories with extra detail in the table Overview from the bar chart, specifics from the table

Units. Keep each unit with its own measure. Never write visitors rose to 70,000 while the temperature reached 20,000: say 70,000 visitors and 20°C, each in its own place.

B. How to analyse mixed and multiple charts

  1. Read each visual on its own. Note the title, unit and period for both. Run the five-look method twice, quickly.
  2. Find the main feature of each in a line: a peak, a trend, the biggest category.
  3. Find the link. Do they share an axis (here, the months)? Write one sentence: visitors follow the temperature.
  4. Mark agreement and exception. Where do both move together? Where does one move and the other does not?
  5. Choose the organisation. If the link is strong, organise by period or theme and mention both visuals in each paragraph. If the link is weak, give one body paragraph to each visual.
  6. Select about six to eight figures from each visual, and no more than about fourteen in all.

A worked analysis for the charts below:

Feature Visitors (bar chart) Temperature (line graph)
Lowest point January January
Highest point August August
Pattern Rises from February to August, then falls to November Rises from January to August, then falls to December
Exception Rises slightly in December Falls in December

Both measures have the same low and the same peak and move together almost everywhere. The one month where they move in opposite directions (December) is the feature that earns Band 7 in Task Achievement.

Language for linking two visuals. followed the same pattern as, rose in line with, mirrored, in step with, at the same time, while, whereas, even though, despite, the only exception was.

C. Writing structure for mixed and multiple charts

Paragraph What to write Why
Introduction Paraphrase both visuals, in one or two sentences joined by while or and Shows you understood that they belong together
Overview The main feature of each visual, the link between them, and the exception The big picture of a mixed task is the relationship
Body 1 The first period (or theme), with figures from both visuals Keeps the link visible
Body 2 The second period, including the exception, with figures from both visuals Completes the story

Two-visual tasks need a little more writing: aim for 180 to 200 words. Spend about four minutes looking at both visuals (instead of three), and keep the same 20 minutes in total by writing the introduction and overview in one go.

D. Band 7+ model answer

The bar chart below shows the number of visitors to a coastal town in each month of 2024, and the line graph shows the average temperature in the town over the same period.

Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.

Write at least 150 words. (About 20 minutes)

Model answer · 193 words

The bar chart shows how many people visited a coastal town in each month of 2024, while the line graph gives the average monthly temperature there over the same period.

Overall, visitor numbers followed the temperature closely: both were lowest in winter, rose steadily towards the summer and peaked in August before falling again in the autumn. The only clear exception was December, when visitor numbers increased even though temperatures fell.

In the first three months of the year, the town was cold and quiet. Temperatures stayed below 10°C, and there were no more than 22,000 visitors a month, with only 12,000 in January. From April, however, both figures climbed quickly, and in June the temperature reached 20°C while the number of visitors rose to 70,000.

Both peaked in August, with the temperature reaching 24°C and the number of visitors 102,000, which was more than eight times the January figure for visitors. After the summer, visitor numbers fell to 30,000 in October and to just 15,000 in November, as the temperature dropped to 13°C and then 8°C. In December, however, visitors rose slightly to 18,000 even though the temperature fell further to 5°C.

E. The model answer explained

Paragraph 1: introduction

Sentence Why it works
“The bar chart shows how many people visited a coastal town in each month of 2024, while the line graph gives the average monthly temperature there over the same period.” While joins the two visuals into one sentence, which signals that they belong together. Gives replaces a second shows, and there and the same period avoid repeating the town and the year.

Paragraph 2: overview

Sentence Why it works
“Overall, visitor numbers followed the temperature closely: both were lowest in winter, rose steadily towards the summer and peaked in August before falling again in the autumn.” The link is stated first (followed … closely), and the colon introduces three shared features. Both compresses two visuals into one clause. No figures are needed.
“The only clear exception was December, when visitor numbers increased even though temperatures fell.” The disagreement between the two visuals, in the overview where the examiner looks for it. Even though shows the contrast.

Paragraph 3: the first period

Sentence Why it works
“In the first three months of the year, the town was cold and quiet.” A plain-English sentence that sums up both visuals before any figures appear, which helps the reader.
“Temperatures stayed below 10°C, and there were no more than 22,000 visitors a month, with only 12,000 in January.” Each unit stays with its own measure (°C with temperature, thousands with visitors). No more than gives the upper limit for three months in one phrase.
“From April, however, both figures climbed quickly, and in June the temperature reached 20°C while the number of visitors rose to 70,000.” Both figures is a neutral noun for two different measures. While pairs the two measures at the same date, which is the comparison between visuals that a mixed task needs.

Paragraph 4: the peak, the decline and the exception

Sentence Why it works
“Both peaked in August, with the temperature reaching 24°C and the number of visitors 102,000, which was more than eight times the January figure for visitors.” The order (temperature, then visitors) is made clear by naming each measure, so both is never ambiguous. The relative clause gives a ratio (102 ÷ 12 = 8.5) for the visitors only.
“After the summer, visitor numbers fell to 30,000 in October and to just 15,000 in November, as the temperature dropped to 13°C and then 8°C.” As links the two falls without claiming one caused the other. Two months are covered in one sentence by pairing and then.
“In December, however, visitors rose slightly to 18,000 even though the temperature fell further to 5°C.” The exception from the overview is now given its figures. Slightly matches a rise of three thousand on a scale of 120, and fell further shows the temperature continued to drop.

How the answer meets the four criteria

Criterion Evidence in the answer
Task Achievement Both visuals are described, the link is stated, and the exception is selected and supported; the units and figures are accurate; no reasons (such as holidays) are invented.
Coherence and Cohesion Organised by period with both visuals in each paragraph, not chart by chart; while, however, even though, as, both; figures and the same period as reference words.
Lexical Resource followed … closely, peaked, climbed quickly, the only clear exception, no more than, dropped; paraphrase (gives for shows, visitor numbers for number of visitors).
Grammatical Range and Accuracy Past simple; participle clauses (with the temperature reaching); relative clause (which was more than eight times); concession (even though); correct comparison (more than eight times the January figure).
