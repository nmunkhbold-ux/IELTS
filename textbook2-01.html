Module 1: The test, the four criteria and a 40-minute plan

Task 2 decides most of your Writing score, so learn what the examiner rewards before you learn to write. This module explains the task, the four criteria, the features that limit a band, and a timed plan you can use for every essay.

A. What Task 2 asks you to do

Task 2
Time About 40 minutes (the whole Writing test is 60 minutes)
Length At least 250 words. Aim for 270 to 320
Weight Two thirds of the Writing mark. Task 1 is one third
What you get A topic, followed by a point of view, an argument or a problem, followed by one or two questions
What you write An essay in full paragraphs, with no bullet points or sub-headings
Style Academic or semi-formal. No slang, no chatty phrases
Content Your own reasons and relevant examples from your knowledge or experience. No specialist knowledge is needed

A Task 2 question always has the same shape. Here is an original example with its parts labelled.

Many people now buy their food from supermarkets instead of from local markets. [topic and point of view]

Do you think this is a positive or a negative development? [question]

Give reasons for your answer and include any relevant examples from your own knowledge or experience. [standard instruction]

Write at least 250 words.

Two things about the question matter from the start:

  • Answer every main part. The question above has one main part (positive or negative?) but it also needs you to say which one you believe. A question with two questions has two main parts, and both must be answered.
  • Keep to the topic given. If the question is about food shopping, an essay about the history of farming will not score well, however good the English is.

How long is long enough? IELTS does not publish a penalty for each missing word. But a short essay gives the examiner little evidence of developed ideas, range and accuracy, and an answer of 20 words or fewer is Band 1 on every criterion. Practise writing 270 to 320 words in the time. Writing more is not penalised, but a longer essay has more places to make mistakes.

Paper or computer? The task is the same. On the computer-delivered test you see a running word count. On paper you must count your own words, so practise counting by lines: find out how many words you write on an average line, then multiply.

Seven myths about Task 2

Myth The truth
"You must write exactly four paragraphs." No set number exists. Paragraphs are expected, and each should have one clear topic. Four is the safest plan (Module 4)
"Longer is always better." Only if the extra words develop your ideas. Padding and repetition do not help
"A memorised template scores well." A totally memorised answer can be given Band 0, and memorised phrases used in the wrong place weaken Lexical Resource
"Difficult words always raise your score." Precise words used correctly raise it. A rare word used wrongly lowers it
"You must agree with the question." You can take any position, as long as it is clear and supported
"You need facts and statistics." You need relevant reasons and examples. Invented statistics are not needed, and general examples are fine
"Short, simple sentences are safe." They are safe, but they cannot give you Band 7. You need a variety of complex structures

B. The four criteria in plain English

Each criterion is worth 25% of your Task 2 score. The examiner gives a band for each one and averages them. The table below puts the official descriptors into plain words for Bands 6 and 7. Read the full descriptors yourself too: IELTS Writing band descriptors (PDF).

Criterion The examiner asks Band 6 Band 7 Quick test
Task Response (TR) Did you answer the whole question, with a clear position and developed ideas? All main parts are answered, some more fully than others. The position is relevant. Some ideas are thin or not well supported, and the conclusion may be unclear All main parts are answered and the position is clear throughout. Main ideas are extended and supported, though some support may be general or lose focus Underline each question in the prompt. Point to the sentence that answers each. State your position in one sentence
Coherence and Cohesion (CC) Can the reader follow your argument easily? Clear overall progression, but linking can be mechanical or faulty, and paragraphing is not always logical Ideas are logically organised and progress clearly throughout. A range of linking devices is used, with some over- or under-use. Each paragraph has a clear central topic Write each paragraph's topic in five words. Does each paragraph have just one?
Lexical Resource (LR) Is your vocabulary wide, precise and accurate? Enough vocabulary for the task, with some imprecise or repeated words. Some risks with less common words lead to errors Enough range for some flexibility and precision. Some less common words and collocations, with a sense of style. A few errors in word choice or spelling Find six topic collocations (such as raise awareness, reduce congestion). Check their spelling
Grammatical Range and Accuracy (GRA) Is your grammar varied and correct? A mix of simple and complex sentences, but complex ones often contain errors A variety of complex structures. Many sentences have no errors. Good control of grammar and punctuation, with a few errors In two paragraphs, count the sentences with no errors. For Band 7, many should be clean

What "complex structures" means. The official guidance names four kinds for Band 7 and above: passive forms, modal verbs, comparative structures and complex noun phrases. Complex sentences (a main clause plus a subordinate clause) are expected from Band 6. Module 12 teaches all of these.

What separates Band 7 from Band 8. At Band 8, the majority of your sentences are error-free and the errors that remain are occasional, not repeated. A repeated error (for example, always missing the) is called systematic. It does not matter for Band 7, but it matters for Band 8.

Features that limit a band. The official descriptors print some negative features in bold. IELTS says these limit the rating for that criterion. In practice, treat each of the following as a ceiling for the criterion it belongs to. (The exact ceiling is our reading of the descriptors, not an IELTS rule.)

If this happens The criterion that suffers It stays at
A main part of the question is only partly answered Task Response Band 5
Paragraphing is missing or inadequate Coherence and Cohesion Band 5
Spelling or word-formation errors are noticeable and cause difficulty Lexical Resource Band 5
Errors are frequent and cause difficulty for the reader Grammatical Range and Accuracy Band 5
The format does not suit the task (for example, a letter or a list) Task Response Band 4
The vocabulary is unrelated to the task Lexical Resource Band 4
Almost all sentences are simple, with few subordinate clauses Grammatical Range and Accuracy Band 4
The answer has 20 words or fewer All four Band 1

The first row is the most important for students aiming at Band 7. Missing one part of the question costs you more than any grammar mistake. Module 2 teaches you to find every part.

C. A Band 7 essay in sixteen statements

Use this list now to understand the target. Module 16 has the full checklist for marking your own essays.

Task Response

  1. Every main part of the question is answered.
  2. My position is clear in the introduction and still clear in the conclusion.
  3. Each main idea is explained, not just stated.
  4. Each main idea has an example or a specific detail.

Coherence and Cohesion

  1. The essay has an introduction, body paragraphs and a conclusion.
  2. Each body paragraph is about one main idea.
  3. Each paragraph starts with a clear topic sentence.
  4. Linking words show real relationships (contrast, reason, result) and are not used in every sentence.

Lexical Resource

  1. I use topic words precisely instead of general words such as thing and good.
  2. I use at least six natural collocations.
  3. I do not repeat the same key word more than three or four times (I use synonyms and pronouns).
  4. My spelling of common words is correct.

Grammatical Range and Accuracy

  1. I use a mix of simple, compound and complex sentences.
  2. I use some passive forms, modal verbs, comparatives or relative clauses.
  3. Most of my sentences have no errors.
  4. My punctuation is clear: full stops, commas after introductory phrases, no comma splices.

D. The 40-minute plan

Use this plan for every practice essay from now on, so that it is automatic on test day.

Minutes Step What to do
0–3 Read Read the question twice. Underline the topic, the focus and every question word. Decide the question type (Module 2)
3–8 Plan Write your position. Choose two main ideas. Give each one an explanation and an example (Module 3). Write the plan as short notes
8–12 Introduction Paraphrase the topic. State your position or outline the essay
12–20 Body paragraph 1 Topic sentence, explanation, example, link back to the question
20–28 Body paragraph 2 The same pattern with your second idea
28–32 Conclusion Restate your position and summarise the main reasons. Add no new ideas
32–35 Spare time Use it to finish a paragraph or add an example. If you are on time, begin checking
35–40 Check Use the five-minute check below

The five-minute check

  1. Question: have I answered every part of it?
  2. Position: is it the same in the introduction and the conclusion?
  3. Paragraphs: does each one have a topic sentence?
  4. Your three errors: check for the three mistakes you make most often (your error log, Module 15). For many students these are articles, singular and plural endings and subject–verb agreement.
  5. Length and spelling: is the essay at least 250 words? Are the key topic words spelled correctly?

The whole hour. Do not borrow time from Task 2 to improve Task 1. A student who spends 30 minutes on Task 1 and 30 on Task 2 gives up marks where each minute is worth twice as much.

If you are running out of time, cut your second example, not your conclusion. A short conclusion that restates your position (even one sentence) is better than an essay that stops in the middle of an idea.

E. Check yourself

Write T (true) or F (false). Do not look back until you have finished.

  1. Task 2 is worth the same as Task 1.
  2. The four criteria all count equally.
  3. You must write exactly four paragraphs.
  4. Bullet points are acceptable in a Task 2 answer if the points are short and clear.
  5. If you answer only part of one main question, your Task Response score is limited.
  6. A totally memorised answer can receive Band 0.
  7. Examiners expect an academic or semi-formal style.
  8. You need specialist knowledge of the topic to score Band 7.
Show the answer key
  1. F. Task 2 is worth two thirds of the Writing mark and Task 1 one third.
  2. T. Each criterion is 25%.
  3. F. No set number exists. Four is a safe plan, but the examiner expects paragraphs, each with one clear topic.
  4. F. The answer must be written in full, with no bullet points or sub-headings.
  5. T. A main part only partly answered keeps Task Response at Band 5.
  6. T. This is the lowest band for a totally memorised answer.
  7. T. The essay is academic or semi-formal in style.
  8. F. You support your ideas with reasons and examples from your own knowledge or experience.

F. Your starting point

Before you read Module 2, write one essay so that you can measure your progress. Do it under exam conditions: 40 minutes, no dictionary, no phone. Keep the essay. You will mark it again at the end of Module 16 and see how far you have come.

Some people believe that children should learn to cook at school. Others think this is the parents' job.

Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

Give reasons for your answer and include any relevant examples from your own knowledge or experience.

Write at least 250 words.

Afterwards, mark it with the sixteen statements in part C. Give yourself one point for each statement that is true, then record your results in a progress log like this one. Copy it into your notebook and add a row after every essay.
