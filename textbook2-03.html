Module 3: Ideas, examples and planning

"I don't know what to write" is the most common problem in Task 2, and it has a simple cause: students wait for ideas to arrive. This module gives you tools that produce ideas on demand, a pattern for developing each idea so that it is extended and supported, and a five-minute plan.

  • B. Extending and supporting an idea
  • C. Choosing your position and your ideas
  • D. The five-minute plan

A. Where ideas come from

You do not need to be an expert. The examiner wants relevant reasons and examples from your own knowledge or experience, not specialist facts. Use these five idea tools.

Tool 1: Who is affected? Ask who is helped or harmed: individuals, families, children, workers, businesses, communities, the government, the environment.

Tool 2: Which area? Look at the topic from several areas. Each area gives a different kind of idea.

Area Questions to ask Example for "free entry to museums"
Money Who pays? Who saves? What does it cost? Families on low incomes can visit; the government loses ticket income
Education What do people learn? Who has the chance? Children see real objects instead of pictures in books
Health Does it affect body, mind, stress? Quiet places to visit reduce stress
Society Is it fair? How does it affect relationships? Everyone can share the same culture, not only the rich
Environment What is the effect on nature and cities? More visitors may crowd the city centre
Technology How does it change what is possible? Online exhibitions reach people who cannot travel
Time What happens now, and later? In the short term visitor numbers rise; in the long term interest in history grows

Tool 3: For and against. Make two short lists, one for each side. Even if you are certain of your opinion, the other list shows you what to argue against.

Tool 4: The "Why? So what?" chain. Take any idea and ask Why? and So what? twice. Each answer is a new sentence for your paragraph.

Idea: Free museums help children. Why? Because poor families cannot afford tickets. So what? Their children miss chances to see history in real life. So what? They may become less interested in learning.

Tool 5: Compare places and times. How is this different in a city and a village, or now and fifty years ago? Comparisons are easy to explain and give you examples.

B. Extending and supporting an idea

The descriptors for Band 7 say that main ideas must be extended (explained in more detail) and supported (backed with an example or specific evidence). A stated idea alone is not enough. Use the IEER pattern for every main idea.

Letter Step Question it answers Typical words
I Idea What is my point? One reason is that... / A main advantage is...
E Explain Why is this true? How does it work? This is because... / In other words...
E Example Can I show it with a case? For instance... / A good example is...
R Result So what? How does it answer the question? As a result... / Therefore...

From thin to developed

Thin: Free museums are good for education.

Developed: One reason is that free entry gives more children the chance to learn. Families on low incomes often cannot afford tickets for several people, so their children miss the chance to see real objects from history. In cities where museums are free, for instance, school groups can visit several times a year instead of once. As a result, more children develop an interest in learning outside the classroom.

The developed version has four sentences: an idea, an explanation, an example and a result. It is about 70 words, which is the right length for one idea in a body paragraph.

Types of support

Type What it looks like Frame
Reason A cause This is because...
Explanation A clearer version of the idea In other words... / This means that...
General example A typical case For instance, a student who works part-time...
Specific example A named place, country or situation you know well In Mongolia, for example, ...
Personal example Something you saw or did When I was at school, ...
Comparison Two cases side by side Unlike..., ...
Consequence What follows As a result, ... / This can lead to...

Rules for good examples

  • Relevant. The example must prove the idea in the same paragraph.
  • Specific enough. Many people is weak. Families with several children is better.
  • True and safe. Do not invent statistics or the names of studies. Use examples you are sure about, or general examples that do not need figures.
  • Short. One or two sentences. Do not tell a long story.

Avoiding over-generalisation. The Band 7 descriptors mention a tendency to over-generalise. Check the strength of your claims.

Too general More precise
Everyone uses smartphones. Most teenagers in cities own a smartphone.
Technology always makes life better. Technology can save time, but it can also reduce face-to-face contact.
Young people never read. Many young people read less than previous generations did.
Cities are terrible places to live. Large cities often suffer from traffic, noise and high rents.
Exams are useless. Exams can create stress and may not measure practical skills.
Governments never listen. Governments sometimes make decisions without enough public discussion.

C. Choosing your position and your ideas

  1. Use the language test. In the first two minutes of planning, ask: "Which side can I explain in clear English with words I know?" Choose that side, even if it is not the one you believe most strongly. The examiner marks your reasoning and language, not your opinion.
  2. Choose two main ideas, not three or four. Two developed ideas are better than four thin ones. Add a third only for a very simple question.
  3. Check each idea with four questions.
Question If the answer is no...
Does it answer the focus of the question? Throw it away
Is it different from my other idea? Combine them or replace one
Can I explain it in two sentences? Choose an easier idea
Do I have an example? Think of a simple general example, or choose another idea
  1. Take a clear position. In an opinion essay you may "partly agree", but you must say exactly which part you accept. For example: I agree that schools should teach money skills, but I do not believe the subject needs to be compulsory. A position that changes halfway through the essay is a common reason for a low Task Response score.

D. The five-minute plan

Write your plan as notes, not sentences. Use this template.

Notes
Question T / F / I / L from Module 2. Number of main parts
Position One sentence
Introduction Paraphrase of the topic + position
Body 1 Topic sentence / explain / example / result
Body 2 Topic sentence / explain / example / result
Conclusion Position restated + two reasons in brief

A completed plan

Some people think that museums should be free to enter for everyone. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

Notes
Question T: museums. F: free entry for everyone. I: opinion, 1 part. L: everyone
Position I mostly agree: entry should be free, but donations should be encouraged
Introduction Paraphrase: public museums, no charge. Position as above
Body 1 Free entry gives all families a chance to learn. Low-income families cannot pay for several tickets. Example: school groups visit several times a year. Result: more interest in learning
Body 2 Free museums are paid for by the public, so a fair system is needed. Taxes already fund them, and donations can add income. Example: visitors leave small donations at the door. Result: no cost to visitors, but income for the museum
Conclusion Position restated. Free entry = fairness and learning; donations = fair funding

This plan has about 110 words, including the labels. Your own plan can be shorter. If it is much longer, you are writing the essay twice.

F. Try it yourself

Exercise 1. Develop each thin idea into four sentences with the IEER pattern (Idea, Explain, Example, Result).

  1. Exercise is good for health.
  2. Online shopping saves time.
  3. Public transport reduces pollution.
  4. Learning a second language helps you find a job.
Show the answer key

Your wording will differ. These are acceptable models.

  1. Regular exercise helps people to stay healthy. It strengthens the heart and reduces the risk of conditions such as diabetes. For instance, adults who walk briskly for thirty minutes a day are less likely to become seriously ill later in life. As a result, they spend less on medical treatment and miss fewer days of work.
  2. Online shopping saves people a great deal of time. Customers can compare prices and place an order in a few minutes, without travelling to several shops. A busy parent, for example, can buy a week's food during a lunch break. As a result, more time is available for family and rest.
  3. Public transport reduces pollution because one bus carries many people. A bus with fifty passengers removes dozens of cars from the road, and each car would otherwise produce its own exhaust gases. In cities with good metro systems, for example, air quality is often better in the centre. As a result, residents suffer fewer breathing problems.
  4. Speaking a second language gives job seekers an advantage. Many employers need staff who can deal with foreign customers or partners. A graduate who speaks good English, for instance, can apply for jobs in tourism, banking and international companies. As a result, she has more choices and may earn a higher salary.

Exercise 2. Plan each question in five minutes using the template in part D. Write notes only.

  1. Parents should be legally responsible for the bad behaviour of their children. To what extent do you agree or disagree?
  2. Many people feel lonely in big cities. What problems does this cause, and what can be done about it?
  3. More and more people use online banking instead of visiting a bank. What are the advantages and disadvantages?
Show the answer key

Plans vary. Check that yours has all the parts below.

  1. Question: T: parents and children. F: legal responsibility for bad behaviour. I: opinion, 1 part. L: legally, bad behaviour. Position: partly agree. Parents should be responsible for young children, but not for older teenagers. Body 1: young children cannot understand the law, so parents must supervise them (example: a child who damages a shop window). Body 2: older teenagers make their own choices, and punishing parents does not stop them (example: a seventeen-year-old who steals, although his parents warned him). Conclusion: responsibility should depend on age.
  2. Question: T: loneliness in cities. F: problems it causes, and solutions. I: problem and solution, 2 parts. Problems: mental health problems such as depression (example: older people living alone); less community support, so people have nobody to help in an emergency. Solutions: community centres and clubs that bring neighbours together; local governments could organise events and volunteer visits for older people. Conclusion: loneliness harms individuals and communities, but practical local action can reduce it.
  3. Question: T: online banking. F: advantages and disadvantages. I: advantages and disadvantages, 2 parts. Advantage: convenience and speed (example: paying a bill at midnight without queuing); lower costs. Disadvantage: security risks such as fraud (example: fake emails that ask for passwords); people without internet, such as some elderly customers, are excluded. Conclusion: the advantages are real, but safety and fair access need attention.
