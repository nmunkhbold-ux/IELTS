Module 7: Process diagrams, life cycles and flow charts

These three diagram types describe stages and sequence, not trends in data. The shared skills are passive voice, sequencing language and an overview that says how many stages there are and where the process begins and ends.

  • 7.1 Manufacturing processes
  • 7.2 Natural processes
  • 7.3 Life cycle diagrams
  • 7.4 Flow charts

7.1 Manufacturing processes

A. Understanding the question

What it shows. The stages by which a product is made, from raw material to finished product. The visual is a sequence, so the task is about order and grouping, not about trends in data.

How to recognise it. Boxes or pictures joined by arrows, often numbered, with verbs such as is picked, is crushed, is filtered. The task says shows how … is made, is produced or the process of making ….

What to look for. Where the process starts (the raw material) and ends (the product); how many stages there are; the order; any equipment, time or method; any branch (a waste product or by-product); and any stages that happen at the same time.

What the task asks. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features. There is no comparison to make. You describe the stages in order, group them, and say in the overview how many there are and where the process begins and ends.

Common wordings.

Wording What it means for your answer
“The diagram shows how olive oil is produced.” Paraphrase with illustrates how … is produced or the process of producing …
“The pictures show the stages in the manufacture of glass bottles.” Manufacture is a noun: how glass bottles are manufactured
“The flow chart shows how cement is made.” Linear stages, perhaps with a branch
“The diagram shows the process of recycling paper.” Recycling is often a loop or a branch: check the arrows

Tense and voice. A process is a general truth, so use the present simple passive (is picked, are crushed). Use the active only for natural changes or when the doer matters (the paste separates into oil and waste). A process with a past date takes the past simple passive.

Sequencing language. first, then, next, after that, once, before, while, as soon as, finally, in the final stage. Vary the words: firstly, secondly, thirdly on every sentence reads as a list.

B. How to analyse a manufacturing process

  1. Find the start and the end: the raw material and the finished product.
  2. Count the stages (from the numbers or the arrows). Your overview must give the right number.
  3. Check for branches: a by-product or waste stream, or two stages that happen together.
  4. Group the stages. Processes fall into natural groups: preparation, transformation and finishing.
  5. Note the details that add information: a time, a method (by hand or machine), a purpose (to remove dirt).
  6. Decide what to merge. Every stage matters in a process, so none is dropped, but small ones can share a sentence.

A worked grouping for this diagram:

Group Stages What happens
Preparing the olives 1 to 4 Picked, washed, crushed into a paste and mixed
Separating the oil 5 and the by-product Pressed; the solid waste is used as fuel
Finishing the oil 6 and 7 Filtered, bottled and labelled

Ways to join stages in one sentence.

Technique Example
Sequence word + passive First, the olives are picked.
Before or after + -ing They are washed … before being crushed into a paste.
Purpose with to or so that The paste is pressed so that the oil separates.
Relative clause for a by-product … solid waste, which is used as fuel.
While for two things at once While the waste is used as fuel, the oil is filtered.

C. Writing structure for a manufacturing process

Paragraph What to write Why
Introduction Paraphrase the task: what is produced Shows you understand the process
Overview The number of stages, the start and the end, and one key feature such as a by-product or a loop Gives the examiner the shape of the process without the detail
Body 1 The first group of stages, in order, with sequencing words Starts the sequence
Body 2 The remaining stages, including any branch, to the end Completes the sequence

Write in flowing sentences, not “Stage 1 is …, Stage 2 is …”. Never add a stage that is not on the diagram, and never give reasons that are not shown.

D. Band 7+ model answer

The diagram below shows how olive oil is produced.

Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features.

Write at least 150 words. (About 20 minutes)

Model answer · 164 words

The diagram illustrates how olive oil is produced, from the picking of the olives to the bottling of the finished oil.

Overall, the process consists of seven stages, beginning with harvesting and ending with bottling. The olives are first cleaned and crushed, and the oil is then separated from the solid waste, which is not thrown away but used as fuel.

In the first stage, the olives are picked from the trees, either by hand or by machine. They are then washed to remove leaves and dirt before being crushed into a paste. After that, the paste is mixed slowly for about 30 minutes. The first four stages therefore prepare the paste, while the last three deal with the oil.

Next, the paste is pressed so that the oil separates from the solid waste. While the waste is used as fuel, the oil is filtered to remove small particles. In the final stage, the oil is bottled and labelled, and the process is complete.

E. The model answer explained

Paragraph 1: introduction

Sentence Why it works
“The diagram illustrates how olive oil is produced, from the picking of the olives to the bottling of the finished oil.” Illustrates replaces shows. The second half gives the start and end as noun phrases (the picking of, the bottling of), which changes the grammar and previews the whole process.

Paragraph 2: overview

Sentence Why it works
“Overall, the process consists of seven stages, beginning with harvesting and ending with bottling.” It counts the stages correctly and names the start and the end. Harvesting paraphrases picked.
“The olives are first cleaned and crushed, and the oil is then separated from the solid waste, which is not thrown away but used as fuel.” A summary of the shape of the process (clean, crush, separate) and the key feature: the by-product. Not thrown away but used as fuel shows the examiner you understood what the branch means.

Paragraph 3: the first group of stages

Sentence Why it works
“In the first stage, the olives are picked from the trees, either by hand or by machine.” In the first stage orients the reader. Either … or reports the choice shown in the box. The passive is correct because the picker does not matter.
“They are then washed to remove leaves and dirt before being crushed into a paste.” Two stages in one sentence. To remove gives the purpose, and before being crushed (a passive participle) joins stages 2 and 3 without repeating then.
“After that, the paste is mixed slowly for about 30 minutes.” The detail from the box (the time) is reported, and slowly is an adverb of manner. After that varies the sequencing.
“The first four stages therefore prepare the paste, while the last three deal with the oil.” A summary sentence that groups the stages (4 + 3 = 7). It is not in the diagram, but it is true and shows the structure of the process.

Paragraph 4: the second group of stages

Sentence Why it works
“Next, the paste is pressed so that the oil separates from the solid waste.” So that states the purpose. The second clause is active (the oil separates) because separating is a natural change, not something someone does to the oil.
“While the waste is used as fuel, the oil is filtered to remove small particles.” While shows that the two streams continue at the same time, which is the right way to describe a branch.
“In the final stage, the oil is bottled and labelled, and the process is complete.” In the final stage signals the end and mirrors in the first stage, so the sequence is clearly framed.

How the answer meets the four criteria

Criterion Evidence in the answer
Task Achievement A clear overview (seven stages, start, end, by-product); every stage reported in the right order; no invented stages, reasons or opinions.
Coherence and Cohesion Paragraphs by group of stages; varied sequencing (first, then, after that, next, in the final stage); therefore, while and so that link ideas.
Lexical Resource illustrates, consists of, harvesting, crushed, filtered, thrown away, deal with; paraphrase of the diagram's labels.
Grammatical Range and Accuracy Present simple passive controlled throughout; passive participle (before being crushed); infinitive of purpose; so that; relative clause; active and passive used where appropriate.

7.2 Natural processes

A. Understanding the question

What it shows. A sequence of events in nature: how something forms or changes without human action, such as a river delta forming, rain falling, a volcano erupting or coal being created. The method is the same as for a manufacturing process (stages in order), but the actors are natural forces and the time scale is often very long.

How to recognise it. Pictures or boxes showing natural features (rivers, rocks, clouds) joined by arrows, and a task that says shows how … forms, is formed or develops.

What to look for. The starting condition and the final result; the turning point where the key change happens; time words (over thousands of years); and the cause-and-effect links between stages. In a natural process, stages are connected because one causes the next.

What the task asks. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features. Describe the stages in order, say how many there are, and show the key change in the overview.

Common wordings.

Wording What it means for your answer
“The diagram shows how a river delta is formed.” Natural process: no machines and no people
“The diagram illustrates the water cycle.” A cycle, not a line: see 7.3
“The diagrams show how a volcano erupts.” Cause and effect between stages
“The pictures show the formation of coal over millions of years.” Use time phrases such as over millions of years

Tense and voice. Use the present simple. Use the active for natural agents (the river flows, the water drops the sediment) and the passive when the thing affected is the subject (the sediment is deposited). Natural processes use more active verbs than manufacturing processes, because nature does the work.

B. How to analyse a natural process

  1. Find the starting condition and the final result.
  2. Find the agent: what is moving or changing (here, the river and its sediment).
  3. Find the turning point: the stage where the key change occurs (the river slows down).
  4. Find the cause-and-effect links between stages, and the time scale.
  5. Group the stages: here, stages 1 to 3 describe how the sediment arrives and is dropped, and stages 4 to 6 describe how the land builds up.
  6. Stay inside the diagram. Explain how, not why. Do not add scientific reasons the diagram does not give.

Cause-and-effect language.

Purpose Language
Result as a result, consequently, this causes, leading to
Time and cause together when it reaches …, as the river slows …, once the water has slowed …
Long time scale over thousands of years, gradually, eventually, as time passes
New thing appears forms, develops, emerges, builds up

Natural and manufacturing processes compared.

Manufacturing Natural
Doer People and machines Natural forces
Voice Mostly passive Active and passive
Links between stages Order Order and cause
Time Minutes to hours Often years or longer
Overview mentions Stages, start, end, by-product Stages, start, end, turning point, no human activity

C. Writing structure for a natural process

Paragraph What to write Why
Introduction Paraphrase the task, and say it is a natural process Sets the type
Overview The number of stages, that no people are involved, the start, the end and the turning point The big picture of a natural process
Body 1 The early stages, up to the turning point, with the cause and effect Shows how the change begins
Body 2 The later stages and the final result, with time words Shows how the change ends

D. Band 7+ model answer

The diagram below shows how a river delta is formed.

Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features.

Write at least 150 words. (About 20 minutes)

Model answer · 156 words

The diagram shows how a river delta is formed through a natural process that takes place over a very long period.

Overall, the process has six stages and involves no human activity: it begins with sediment moving downstream and ends with the appearance of the delta. The key change happens when the river slows down and starts to drop its sediment.

Firstly, the river flows downhill from the mountains, carrying sand and mud, which are known as sediment. When it reaches the flat land near the sea, the river slows down, and the slower water then drops the sediment at the river mouth. The first three stages therefore describe how the sediment arrives and is dropped, and the last three describe how the new land develops.

As time passes, layers of sediment build up over thousands of years. Eventually, the river divides into smaller channels around the new land, and this land forms a fan-shaped delta.

E. The model answer explained

Paragraph 1: introduction

Sentence Why it works
“The diagram shows how a river delta is formed through a natural process that takes place over a very long period.” The relative clause that takes place over a very long period identifies the type of process and its time scale, so the introduction says more than a bare paraphrase.

Paragraph 2: overview

Sentence Why it works
“Overall, the process has six stages and involves no human activity: it begins with sediment moving downstream and ends with the appearance of the delta.” The count of stages, the point that no people are involved, and the start and end in one sentence. The colon introduces the start and end neatly.
“The key change happens when the river slows down and starts to drop its sediment.” The turning point, named in the overview. It is the idea that links stages 2 and 3, and a high-band answer states it early.

Paragraph 3: the early stages

Sentence Why it works
“Firstly, the river flows downhill from the mountains, carrying sand and mud, which are known as sediment.” The active voice is right because the river is the agent. A participle clause (carrying) adds a detail, and a relative clause (which are known as) defines the key term.
“When it reaches the flat land near the sea, the river slows down, and the slower water then drops the sediment at the river mouth.” When sets up time and cause in one clause. The slower water then drops keeps the cause-and-effect chain from the diagram without adding a reason that is not shown.
“The first three stages therefore describe how the sediment arrives and is dropped, and the last three describe how the new land develops.” A grouping sentence (3 + 3 = 6) that shows the structure of the process. Is dropped is a correct passive for the sediment.

Paragraph 4: the later stages

Sentence Why it works
“As time passes, layers of sediment build up over thousands of years.” A time phrase at the start and build up, an active verb for a natural change.
“Eventually, the river divides into smaller channels around the new land, and this land forms a fan-shaped delta.” Eventually marks the end of a long process; this land is a reference phrase that avoids repeating the new land; fan-shaped delta uses the diagram's own description.

How the answer meets the four criteria

Criterion Evidence in the answer
Task Achievement A clear overview (stages, no human activity, start, end, turning point); every stage reported in order; no invented reasons or details.
Coherence and Cohesion Paragraphs by phase of the process; firstly, when, then, therefore, as time passes, eventually; it, this land as reference words.
Lexical Resource sediment, deposit-type verbs (drops), builds up, divides into, fan-shaped, over a very long period; a defined technical term.
Grammatical Range and Accuracy Present simple, both active and passive; participle clause (carrying); relative clauses; when clause; a colon; correct plural agreement (layers … build up).

7.3 Life cycle diagrams

A. Understanding the question

What it shows. The stages of a living thing's life (an animal, an insect, a plant), arranged so that the last stage leads back to the first. A cycle has no real beginning or end: each generation produces the next.

How to recognise it. Stages joined by arrows in a ring or a loop, often with a closing arrow from the last stage to the first. The task says shows the life cycle of … or the stages in the development of ….

What to look for. Where to start (usually the egg or seed); how many stages there are; the direction of the arrows; the big changes in form or habitat; and where the cycle closes.

What the task asks. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features. Describe the stages in order, make clear that the cycle repeats, and identify the biggest change.

Common wordings.

Wording What it means for your answer
“The diagram shows the life cycle of a frog.” Paraphrase with illustrates and the stages in the life of a frog
“The diagram shows the stages in the development of a butterfly.” A change of form at each stage: name each form
“The diagram illustrates the life cycle of a flowering plant.” A cycle with seeds: say what returns to the start
“The diagram shows the water cycle.” A natural cycle with no living thing: use the same method

Tense and voice. Present simple. The active is common (the eggs hatch, the tail shrinks), and the passive is used for things done to the animal (the eggs are laid in water).

Language for a cycle. the cycle begins with, hatches into, develops into, turns into, grows, at this point, at this stage, then the cycle begins again, continuous, the next generation.

No clear starting point. If the diagram has no start, choose the stage that makes the clearest story (the egg or the seed) and say so: the cycle can be described as starting when ….

B. How to analyse a life cycle

  1. Choose and state the starting stage.
  2. Follow the arrows in order and count the stages.
  3. Identify the biggest change. Here, tadpole to frog (from breathing through gills to having lungs, and from water to land).
  4. Check the closing link. What returns to the start? Here, adult frogs return to the water to mate and lay eggs.
  5. Note habitat, shape and time details that the diagram gives.
  6. Group the stages by what happens to the animal.

A worked grouping for this diagram:

Group Stages What happens
In the water as eggs and tadpoles 1 to 3 Eggs hatch; tadpoles breathe through gills, swim, and grow legs
The change 4 The tail shrinks and lungs develop
Frog 5 and 6 The young frog leaves the water; adults return to the water to mate

Three habits for cycles. Say that the cycle repeats in the overview and again in your last sentence. Use the next generation or new to show that the loop closes. Avoid the end, because a cycle has no end.

C. Writing structure for a life cycle

Paragraph What to write Why
Introduction Paraphrase the task; give the first and last stage Sets the loop
Overview The number of stages, that the cycle is continuous, and the biggest change The shape of a cycle
Body 1 The stages up to the big change, starting from the stage you name Starts the story
Body 2 The stages after the change, and the return to the beginning Closes the loop

D. Band 7+ model answer

The diagram below shows the life cycle of a frog.

Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features.

Write at least 150 words. (About 20 minutes)

Model answer · 157 words

The diagram illustrates the life cycle of a frog, from the laying of eggs to the return of adult frogs to the water.

Overall, the cycle has six stages and is continuous: adult frogs lay eggs, and the animals that develop from them eventually repeat the process. The most important change occurs in the middle stages, when the tadpole, which breathes through gills, develops lungs and becomes a young frog.

The cycle starts when adult frogs lay eggs, known as frogspawn, in water. The eggs hatch into tadpoles, which breathe through gills and swim using a tail. Next, the tadpoles grow back legs first and front legs afterwards.

After that, the tail shrinks and lungs develop, so the animal becomes a young frog. At this point, it leaves the water, and as an adult it lives both on land and in water. Finally, the adult frogs return to the water to mate, and the cycle begins again.

E. The model answer explained

Paragraph 1: introduction

Sentence Why it works
“The diagram illustrates the life cycle of a frog, from the laying of eggs to the return of adult frogs to the water.” The second half names the first and last stages as noun phrases (the laying of, the return of), which previews the loop: the last stage leads back to the first.

Paragraph 2: overview

Sentence Why it works
“Overall, the cycle has six stages and is continuous: adult frogs lay eggs, and the animals that develop from them eventually repeat the process.” The count, and the key feature of a cycle: it is continuous. The animals that develop from them means the next generation without using the word.
“The most important change occurs in the middle stages, when the tadpole, which breathes through gills, develops lungs and becomes a young frog.” The biggest change is identified in the overview. The relative clause which breathes through gills names the old form, and develops lungs the new one, so the change is clear.

Paragraph 3: the early stages

Sentence Why it works
“The cycle starts when adult frogs lay eggs, known as frogspawn, in water.” The starting point is chosen and stated. Known as frogspawn is a reduced relative clause (which are known as) that keeps the diagram's technical term.
“The eggs hatch into tadpoles, which breathe through gills and swim using a tail.” Hatch into is the precise verb for the change of form. A relative clause adds the two features from the diagram.
“Next, the tadpoles grow back legs first and front legs afterwards.” First … afterwards shows the order inside a single stage, a detail many students miss.

Paragraph 4: after the change, and the loop

Sentence Why it works
“After that, the tail shrinks and lungs develop, so the animal becomes a young frog.” So shows that the two changes together produce the young frog. The active voice is right because these are natural changes.
“At this point, it leaves the water, and as an adult it lives both on land and in water.” At this point marks the turning point; both … and describes the adult habitat accurately.
“Finally, the adult frogs return to the water to mate, and the cycle begins again.” The last clause closes the loop explicitly, as the closing arrow on the diagram does. Note that the sentence does not say the end.

How the answer meets the four criteria

Criterion Evidence in the answer
Task Achievement A clear overview (six stages, continuous, the big change); every stage in order; the loop closed; nothing invented.
Coherence and Cohesion Paragraphs before and after the big change; next, after that, at this point, finally; they, it, this point as reference; the loop closes.
Lexical Resource hatch into, breathe through gills, lungs, frogspawn, continuous, return to; precise technical words.
Grammatical Range and Accuracy Present simple active and passive; relative clauses (which breathes, known as); so; when clause; correct agreement (tadpoles … grow, the tail shrinks).

7.4 Flow charts

A. Understanding the question

What it shows. A procedure in which the path depends on answers. Boxes are steps, diamonds are decisions with yes and no exits, and arrows show the route. Unlike a process diagram, a flow chart can end in several different ways.

How to recognise it. Diamonds containing questions, arrows labelled yes or no, and more than one end point. The task says shows how a decision is made, the procedure for … or how … is handled.

What to look for. The start; every decision and its options; every possible end point; how many decisions and outcomes there are; the shortest and the longest routes; and any loop (a no that sends you back).

What the task asks. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features. Describe the main route and the alternatives, and make the number of decisions and outcomes clear in the overview.

Common wordings.

Wording What it means for your answer
“The flow chart shows how a refund request is handled.” Describe the procedure and its different outcomes
“The diagram shows how a loan application is assessed.” Several decisions, so use conditional language
“The flow chart illustrates how students are admitted to a course.” The passive for what is done to the students
“The chart shows the procedure for dealing with a complaint.” A procedure may loop back: look for a no that returns

Tense and voice. Present simple, mostly passive for steps (the item is inspected). Use conditional structures for the decisions.

Language for decisions. if …, …, when …, if not, otherwise, unless, only if, depending on whether …, in that case, in other words.

B. How to analyse a flow chart

  1. Find the start.
  2. List the decisions and their two exits.
  3. Follow every path to its end and write the outcome.
  4. Count: the decisions, the outcomes, and the shortest and longest routes.
  5. Group: the main path, and the branches that leave it.
  6. Plan the order: describe the shortest branch first so that you can clear it away, then follow the main path to the end.

A worked list of paths for this chart:

Path Route Outcome
A Request, first decision: no The request is refused
B Request, first decision: yes, item sent back, inspected, second decision: yes A full refund is paid
C The same, but the second decision: no A refund minus a postage fee is paid

Three paths and two decisions. The shortest route (path A) needs only one decision; the longest routes (paths B and C) need two decisions and two extra steps (sending the item back and inspecting it).

Conditional language.

Purpose Example
A decision and its consequence If the purchase was recent, the customer sends the item back.
The other option If it was not, the request is refused. / Otherwise, …
A condition on one outcome A full refund is paid only if the item is faulty.
A summary of the logic In other words, the amount depends on the condition of the item.

C. Writing structure for a flow chart

Paragraph What to write Why
Introduction Paraphrase the task: what procedure and by whom Shows you understand the chart
Overview The number of decisions, and the possible outcomes The big picture of a branching chart
Body 1 The start, the first decision and the shortest branch Clears the simplest path first
Body 2 The main path through the remaining steps, and the final outcomes Completes every path

D. Band 7+ model answer

The flow chart below shows how an online shop handles a refund request.

Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features.

Write at least 150 words. (About 20 minutes)

Model answer · 162 words

The flow chart illustrates the procedure that an online shop follows when a customer asks for a refund.

Overall, the procedure includes two decisions and can end in one of three ways: the request is refused, the customer receives a full refund, or the refund is reduced by a postage fee.

The process begins when a customer requests a refund. The shop first checks whether the item was bought within the previous 30 days. If it was not, the request is refused and the process ends there.

If the purchase was recent, the customer sends the item back, and the shop inspects it. When the item is faulty, a full refund is paid. Otherwise, a refund is paid from which a $5 postage fee is deducted, so only customers with faulty items are refunded in full. In other words, a refund is only possible for purchases made in the last 30 days, and the amount depends on the condition of the item.

E. The model answer explained

Paragraph 1: introduction

Sentence Why it works
“The flow chart illustrates the procedure that an online shop follows when a customer asks for a refund.” The task says shows how … handles a refund request; the answer restates it with illustrates the procedure that … follows and asks for a refund. The relative clause names who follows the procedure and when.

Paragraph 2: overview

Sentence Why it works
“Overall, the procedure includes two decisions and can end in one of three ways: the request is refused, the customer receives a full refund, or the refund is reduced by a postage fee.” The two numbers a flow chart overview needs (decisions and outcomes), then the three outcomes in a colon list. The list is in parallel form, so it reads cleanly.

Paragraph 3: the start and the shortest branch

Sentence Why it works
“The process begins when a customer requests a refund.” The start is named.
“The shop first checks whether the item was bought within the previous 30 days.” The decision is turned from a question into a whether clause, which is the standard way to report a decision. First signals the order.
“If it was not, the request is refused and the process ends there.” The no branch, reported with if and clearly finished (ends there). It is the shortest path, so it is cleared away before the main path.

Paragraph 4: the main path and the two outcomes

Sentence Why it works
“If the purchase was recent, the customer sends the item back, and the shop inspects it.” The yes branch, with two steps in one sentence. The active voice is used because both doers (customer, shop) are named in the chart.
“When the item is faulty, a full refund is paid. Otherwise, a refund is paid from which a $5 postage fee is deducted, so only customers with faulty items are refunded in full.” The second decision, with when for yes and otherwise for no. From which … is deducted is a formal relative clause with a passive, and so only … states a consequence.
“In other words, a refund is only possible for purchases made in the last 30 days, and the amount depends on the condition of the item.” A summary of the logic of the whole chart. It is accurate and adds no information that is not on the chart.

How the answer meets the four criteria

Criterion Evidence in the answer
Task Achievement A clear overview with the number of decisions and outcomes; every path followed to its end; nothing invented.
Coherence and Cohesion Shortest branch first, then the main path; first, if, when, otherwise, so, in other words; it, there as reference words.
Lexical Resource procedure, requests, inspects, deducted, refunded in full, postage fee; decision words reported correctly with whether.
Grammatical Range and Accuracy Conditionals with if, when and otherwise; passive (is refused, is deducted); a formal relative clause (from which); whether clause; accurate tenses throughout.
