Module 8: Maps, floor plans and building layouts

Map and plan tasks compare two pictures of the same place. The skills are location language (north of, next to, along), change language (was replaced by, will be built) and a clear sense of what stays the same.

  • 8.1 Maps: past and present
  • 8.2 Maps: present and proposed development
  • 8.3 Floor plans and building layouts

8.1 Maps: past and present

A. Understanding the question

What it shows. Two or more maps of the same place at different dates, drawn at the same scale and with the same orientation. They show how land use has changed in a village, a town centre or a campus.

How to recognise it. Maps with years in their titles, showing buildings, roads, rivers and open land, usually with a compass arrow.

What to look for. What stays the same (these anchor features give you reference points); what has been added; what has been removed or replaced; what has changed in size or use; and where each change is.

What the task asks. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant. On a map, the comparison is then against now.

Common wordings.

Wording What it means for your answer
“The maps show the village of Elmbrook in 1995 and 2025.” Both dates are in the past: past simple
“The maps show changes to a town centre between 1980 and 2020.” Describe the changes as a story of development
“The maps show a university campus in 2000 and today.” Today is now: past simple for the old map, present perfect or present for the new one
“The maps show how an area has changed over thirty years.” Present perfect is possible: has been replaced, has expanded

Tense. Let the dates decide. Two past dates take the past simple, with the past perfect for a change that was complete before the later date (by 2025 the farm had been replaced). If the later map is today, use the present perfect (has been replaced) or the present simple.

Change language. was replaced by, was converted into, was demolished, was knocked down, was built, was constructed, was extended, was enlarged, was widened, was created, was cleared, remained unchanged, stayed the same.

Location language.

Purpose Language
Compass in the north-west of, to the east of, south of the main road
Next to something beside, next to, close to, opposite, between … and …
On an old site on the site of, in place of, where the farm used to be
Along a line along the river, along the eastern edge, at the end of the road

B. How to analyse a map

  1. Find the dates and which map is earlier.
  2. Find the anchors: the features that do not change. Use them as reference points for locations.
  3. Compare feature by feature and sort each into one of five types: added, removed, replaced, changed size, unchanged.
  4. Group the changes by theme (housing, shopping and transport, leisure) or by area (north, south).
  5. Choose your location words from the compass and the anchors.
  6. Select. On a map you usually mention almost every change, because there are few, but you group them. Leave out trivial details.

A worked change analysis for this pair of maps:

Feature 1995 2025 Type of change
North-west land Farm and fields Housing estate Replaced
School One building The same building plus an extension Extended
Shop south of the road A small shop A supermarket and a car park Replaced and enlarged
Riverside Empty A park Added
East edge of the village No road A new bypass Added
Main road, church, river Present Present Unchanged

The changes fall naturally into two areas (the north, and the south with the edges), and the unchanged features make a useful contrast for the end.

C. Writing structure for a map task

Paragraph What to write Why
Introduction Paraphrase the task: the place and the two dates Shows you understand the maps
Overview The main kind of change (here, development), two or three of the biggest changes, and what stays the same The big picture of a map
Body 1 The changes in the first area (here, the north), with locations Groups related changes
Body 2 The changes in the second area, then the unchanged features Completes the comparison

Do not describe the first map completely and then the second map. Compare the two maps as soon as you mention a place: a farm … had been replaced by a housing estate.

D. Band 7+ model answer

The maps below show the village of Elmbrook in 1995 and 2025.

Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.

Write at least 150 words. (About 20 minutes)

Model answer · 164 words

The two maps illustrate the changes that took place in the village of Elmbrook between 1995 and 2025.

Overall, the village became much more developed: farmland was replaced by housing, the shopping and school facilities were expanded, and a new road and a park were added. However, the main road, the church and the river remained unchanged.

In 1995, the north-west of the village consisted of a farm surrounded by fields, but by 2025 this land had been completely replaced by a housing estate. The school, which stood in the north-east, was extended with a new building to its south.

South of the main road, the small shop near the houses was demolished and a larger supermarket was built on the site, with a car park to its east. In addition, a park was created beside the river in the south-west, and a new bypass was constructed along the eastern edge of the village. In contrast, the church stayed in exactly the same position.

E. The model answer explained

Paragraph 1: introduction

Sentence Why it works
“The two maps illustrate the changes that took place in the village of Elmbrook between 1995 and 2025.” Illustrate the changes that took place replaces show, and between … and … folds both dates into one phrase.

Paragraph 2: overview

Sentence Why it works
“Overall, the village became much more developed: farmland was replaced by housing, the shopping and school facilities were expanded, and a new road and a park were added.” The main type of change (development) and three of the biggest changes in a colon list, with no locations or details. The passive is correct because the builders are unimportant.
“However, the main road, the church and the river remained unchanged.” The anchors, named in the overview. However signals the contrast, and listing what stays the same shows that you compared the maps carefully.

Paragraph 3: the north

Sentence Why it works
“In 1995, the north-west of the village consisted of a farm surrounded by fields, but by 2025 this land had been completely replaced by a housing estate.” One sentence compares the two maps. Consisted of and a participle phrase (surrounded by) describe the old map; the past perfect passive (had been completely replaced) with by 2025 shows a finished change. This land avoids repeating the north-west.
“The school, which stood in the north-east, was extended with a new building to its south.” A relative clause gives the school's location, and to its south places the extension. The passive was extended states the change without naming who built it.

Paragraph 4: the south and the unchanged features

Sentence Why it works
“South of the main road, the small shop near the houses was demolished and a larger supermarket was built on the site, with a car park to its east.” The main road is used as a reference point for the location. Was demolished and was built on the site describe a replacement in two clauses, and with a car park to its east adds a related new feature.
“In addition, a park was created beside the river in the south-west, and a new bypass was constructed along the eastern edge of the village.” Two additions in one sentence, each with its own location (beside the river, along the eastern edge). Created and constructed are varied choices for built.
“In contrast, the church stayed in exactly the same position.” The unchanged anchor closes the answer, and in contrast ties back to the overview.

How the answer meets the four criteria

Criterion Evidence in the answer
Task Achievement A clear overview naming the kind of change and what stayed the same; every significant change is reported and located correctly; no reasons or opinions are invented.
Coherence and Cohesion Grouped by area (north, south), not map by map; however, by 2025, in addition, in contrast; this land, the site, its as references.
Lexical Resource consisted of, replaced by, extended, demolished, constructed, on the site, along the eastern edge; varied change verbs.
Grammatical Range and Accuracy Past simple passive; past perfect passive (had been replaced); relative clause (which stood); participle (surrounded by); correct prepositions of place.

8.2 Maps: present and proposed development

A. Understanding the question

What it shows. A map of a place as it is now, and a map of how it will look if a plan goes ahead: a redevelopment, a new building programme or a proposed layout. Sometimes there are two competing plans (Plan A and Plan B) and the task is to compare them.

How to recognise it. Titles such as Now and Proposed plan, or at present and in 2035, and words such as plan, proposal, proposed or planned in the task.

What to look for. What will be removed, added, converted or extended; what will stay the same; where each change will happen; and what the overall purpose of the plan seems to be, judged only from what the map shows.

What the task asks. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant. The comparison is between now and the plan.

Common wordings.

Wording What it means for your answer
“The maps show the current layout of a waterfront and a proposal for its redevelopment.” Present for the current map, future for the plan
“The maps show a town centre now and the plan for 2035.” The date is in the future: will
“The diagrams show two proposals for a new airport.” Compare the two plans with each other, as well as with the present
“The maps show the present layout of a park and the changes that are planned.” Are planned, are due, are expected are all possible

Tense. Use the present simple for the existing map (a car park occupies the north) and the future for the plan (the car park will be turned into a public square). Use the passive future for most changes: will be built, will be converted. Stay with will: the map shows a plan, and words such as might or could are only needed if the task says the plan is uncertain.

Plan language. According to the plan, the plan is to …, it is proposed that …, is due to be …, is expected to …, will be demolished, will be replaced by, will be converted into, will be built on the site of, will remain unchanged.

Past and future map language compared.

Meaning Past map (8.1) Future map (8.2)
Something is removed was demolished will be demolished
Something changes use was converted into will be converted into
Something is new was built will be built
Something stays remained unchanged will remain unchanged
Where things are stood, was located will stand, will be located

B. How to analyse a proposal map

  1. Decide which map is now and which is the plan.
  2. Find the anchors. The main road and the café do not change.
  3. List the changes with a verb in the future for each: replaced, converted, built, extended, removed.
  4. Group them by area (north, south) or by type (new buildings, changed uses, new public spaces).
  5. State the overall purpose carefully, using only what the map shows: here, the plan replaces parking and storage with public and tourist uses.
  6. Plan your tenses: present for the current features, future for the plan.

A worked change analysis for these maps:

Feature Now Proposed Type of change
North-east of the waterfront Car park Public square Replaced
North-west Empty land Hotel Built on empty land
South Warehouse Museum Converted
Seafront No walkway Promenade Added
End of the pier No buildings Ferry terminal Added
Main road, café, pier Present Present Unchanged (the pier gains a terminal)

C. Writing structure for a proposal map

Paragraph What to write Why
Introduction Paraphrase the task: the place, the current layout and the proposal Shows you understand the maps
Overview The main change in function (here, parking and storage to public and tourist uses) and what stays The big picture of the plan
Body 1 The present state and the planned change in the first area (north) Compares now with the plan area by area
Body 2 The remaining areas (south, seafront and pier) Completes the comparison

D. Band 7+ model answer

The maps below show the Harbour Bay waterfront now and a plan for its redevelopment.

Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.

Write at least 150 words. (About 20 minutes)

Model answer · 160 words

The maps show the current layout of the Harbour Bay waterfront, which faces the sea to the east, and a proposal for its redevelopment.

Overall, the waterfront will change from an area used mainly for parking and storage into a public and tourist area. The main road and the café will remain where they are, but every other building or open space will be replaced or changed.

At present, a large car park occupies the north of the waterfront, with an area of empty land to its west. According to the plan, the car park will be turned into a public square, and a hotel will be built on the empty land, which lies north of the main road.

In the south, the warehouse will be converted into a museum, and a promenade will be built along the seafront. In addition, a ferry terminal will be constructed at the end of the pier, which currently has no buildings on it.

E. The model answer explained

Paragraph 1: introduction

Sentence Why it works
“The maps show the current layout of the Harbour Bay waterfront, which faces the sea to the east, and a proposal for its redevelopment.” A relative clause adds the orientation (the sea is on the east side), which helps with every later direction. Proposal for its redevelopment turns the verb into a noun phrase, so the grammar changes as well as the vocabulary.

Paragraph 2: overview

Sentence Why it works
“Overall, the waterfront will change from an area used mainly for parking and storage into a public and tourist area.” The change in function, described with from … into and a reduced relative clause (used mainly for). The future will change is the right tense for a plan.
“The main road and the café will remain where they are, but every other building or open space will be replaced or changed.” The anchors, then a generalisation that is true: the car park, the empty land, the warehouse and the pier all change. But signals the contrast.

Paragraph 3: the north

Sentence Why it works
“At present, a large car park occupies the north of the waterfront, with an area of empty land to its west.” At present tells the reader that this is the current map and takes the present simple. A with phrase adds the neighbouring feature without a new sentence.
“According to the plan, the car park will be turned into a public square, and a hotel will be built on the empty land, which lies north of the main road.” According to the plan marks the switch to the future. Two passive futures describe the two changes, and a relative clause locates the hotel against the anchor (the main road).

Paragraph 4: the south, the seafront and the pier

Sentence Why it works
“In the south, the warehouse will be converted into a museum, and a promenade will be built along the seafront.” Converted into is the precise verb for a change of use; built along places the new walkway. The passive future is used throughout.
“In addition, a ferry terminal will be constructed at the end of the pier, which currently has no buildings on it.” Currently returns briefly to the present for the existing map, while the main clause stays in the future, so now and then are compared inside one sentence.

How the answer meets the four criteria

Criterion Evidence in the answer
Task Achievement A clear overview of the change in function and what stays the same; every change is reported and located; nothing outside the maps is claimed.
Coherence and Cohesion Grouped by area, not map by map; at present, according to the plan, in addition; its and which as references; present-then-future structure in each paragraph.
Lexical Resource proposal, redevelopment, occupies, converted into, promenade, constructed, at the end of the pier; varied verbs for change.
Grammatical Range and Accuracy Present simple and passive future used correctly together; relative clauses (which lies, which faces, which currently has); reduced relative (used mainly for); from … into.

8.3 Floor plans and building layouts

A. Understanding the question

What it shows. The layout of a building, a floor or a room: a house, a school, an office, a museum, a library. Most tasks show a before and after (a renovation, an extension, a reorganisation) or a plan and a proposal. Some show a single plan and ask you to describe the layout.

How to recognise it. An outline of rooms with labels, walls and gaps for doors, and a task that says floor plan, layout, ground floor, renovation or extension.

What to look for. The entrance (your anchor); which rooms stay in the same place; walls that are removed or added; rooms that are merged or divided; rooms whose use changes; new doors or exits; and changes in size.

What the task asks. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant. The comparison is before against after, or now against the proposal.

Common wordings.

Wording What it means for your answer
“The plans show the ground floor of a community centre before and after renovation.” Past simple: the changes have been made
“The floor plans show the layout of a house and a proposed extension.” Present for the house, future for the extension
“The diagrams show how a school library was reorganised.” Past simple passive
“The plan shows the layout of a museum.” A single plan: present simple and location language

Tense. Use the past simple for changes already made (the wall was removed), the future for a proposal (a wall will be added), and the present simple to describe a single plan (the entrance leads to a hall).

Change language. was knocked down, was removed, was divided into, was split into, was merged with, was combined with, was converted into, was turned into, was enlarged, was extended, was moved to, was relocated, was added, remained in the same position.

Layout language.

Purpose Language
Compass in the north-west, in the south-east corner, along the north wall
Without a compass on the left, on the right, at the front, at the back, opposite the entrance
Next to something beside, next to, adjacent to, between … and …, near the entrance
Size larger, smaller, twice the size of, takes up the whole north side
Features door, exit, corridor, partition, wall, entrance hall, reception

B. How to analyse a floor plan

  1. Find the entrance and the compass. They are your anchors for every location.
  2. List every room on both plans.
  3. Sort each room: unchanged, changed use, changed size (a wall moved), added, removed or merged.
  4. Notice doors and exits. A new door is a feature even though it is small.
  5. Group the changes by theme (creating space, new uses, unchanged) or by area.
  6. Plan your location words from the compass, from the entrance, or from a neighbouring room.

A worked change analysis for these plans:

Room or feature Before After Type of change
Main hall A large room in the north-west and west The whole north side Enlarged (a wall was removed)
Meeting room (north-east) A separate room Part of the main hall Merged
South part of the hall Part of the hall A café Converted
Office (south-east) An office A meeting room Converted
North wall No door A new exit Added
Kitchen, toilets, entrance hall In place In place Unchanged

Two habits for floor plans. Do not invent sizes: a plan with no scale cannot be described in metres. And describe positions from one fixed point of view: north is up, and the entrance is in the south wall.

C. Writing structure for a floor plan

Paragraph What to write Why
Introduction Paraphrase the task: the building and before and after Shows you understand the plans
Overview The main kind of change (here, more public space) and what stays the same The big picture of the renovation
Body 1 The biggest change (the hall and café) with locations Puts the most important change first
Body 2 The remaining changes, and the rooms that did not change Completes the comparison

D. Band 7+ model answer

The floor plans below show the ground floor of a community centre before and after it was renovated.

Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.

Write at least 150 words. (About 20 minutes)

Model answer · 160 words

The floor plans show the layout of a community centre before and after it was renovated.

Overall, the building was reorganised to create more space for public use: the main hall became much larger, a café was added, and one of the rooms was given a new function. The entrance hall, kitchen and toilets stayed in the same positions.

Originally, the main hall occupied the north-west of the building, with a meeting room beside it in the north-east corner. The wall between them was removed, so the hall stretched across the whole north side. In addition, the southern part of the old hall was converted into a café, which was located next to the entrance hall.

In the south-east, the office was converted into a new meeting room, while the kitchen, which lay to the north of it, was left unchanged. Finally, a new exit was added in the north wall of the hall, and the toilets did not change.

E. The model answer explained

Paragraph 1: introduction

Sentence Why it works
“The floor plans show the layout of a community centre before and after it was renovated.” Layout names what the plans show. Before and after it was renovated turns the task's noun (renovation) into a clause, which changes the structure as well as the words.

Paragraph 2: overview

Sentence Why it works
“Overall, the building was reorganised to create more space for public use: the main hall became much larger, a café was added, and one of the rooms was given a new function.” The main kind of change and three examples in a colon list, with no room positions. Was given a new function covers the office-to-meeting-room change without naming it.
“The entrance hall, kitchen and toilets stayed in the same positions.” The unchanged rooms, named in the overview so that the examiner can see that you compared the two plans fully.

Paragraph 3: the biggest change

Sentence Why it works
“Originally, the main hall occupied the north-west of the building, with a meeting room beside it in the north-east corner.” Originally sets the earlier plan. Compass words fix both rooms, and with … beside it adds the neighbour without a new sentence.
“The wall between them was removed, so the hall stretched across the whole north side.” The change (a wall removed) and its result (so) in one sentence. The passive was removed is correct because who removed it does not matter.
“In addition, the southern part of the old hall was converted into a café, which was located next to the entrance hall.” Converted into is the right verb for a change of use, and the relative clause places the café against a fixed room.

Paragraph 4: the remaining changes

Sentence Why it works
“In the south-east, the office was converted into a new meeting room, while the kitchen, which lay to the north of it, was left unchanged.” A change and an unchanged room in one sentence, joined by while. The relative clause locates the kitchen against the office. Lay is the correct past of lie.
“Finally, a new exit was added in the north wall of the hall, and the toilets did not change.” The small feature (the exit) is not ignored, and the answer ends by closing the list of unchanged rooms.

How the answer meets the four criteria

Criterion Evidence in the answer
Task Achievement A clear overview naming the kind of change and the unchanged rooms; every change is reported and located correctly, including the small new exit; nothing invented.
Coherence and Cohesion Biggest change first; originally, so, in addition, while, finally; them, it, the old hall as references.
Lexical Resource layout, renovated, reorganised, occupied, converted into, stretched across, adjacent-type locations; varied change verbs.
Grammatical Range and Accuracy Past simple passive throughout; relative clauses (which was located, which lay); so result clause; while contrast; correct irregular past (lay).
