Module 1: Foundations of Task 1

Task 1 asks you to turn one visual into a short, accurate report in about 20 minutes, and the skill that earns Band 7 is choosing and comparing the key features, not describing every number.

1.1 The task at a glance

Task 1
Time About 20 minutes (Writing is 60 minutes in total)
Length At least 150 words; aim for 170–190 so a miscount never costs you
Input One visual, or two: a graph, chart, table, map, process or other diagram
Output A report in your own words: what the visual shows, its main features and comparisons
Not allowed Opinions, explanations of causes, predictions that the visual does not show
Weight One third of the Writing score; Task 2 is worth two thirds

1.2 What the examiner marks

There are four criteria, each worth a quarter of the Task 1 score. The table shows what each looks like in a typical pre-intermediate student's writing at Band 6 and at Band 7. Bands in this book are teaching estimates, not official scores.

Criterion Band 6 usually looks like Band 7 looks like
Task Achievement Main features are mentioned, but the overview is missing or unclear, and some figures are wrong or unimportant A clear overview, key features chosen and supported by accurate figures, and comparisons that show how items relate
Coherence and Cohesion Paragraphs exist, but linking words repeat mechanically (firstly, secondly) and ideas are listed, not grouped Each paragraph has one clear purpose; linking is varied and natural, including reference words (this, the latter, respectively)
Lexical Resource Correct but narrow: rise, rise, rise; an occasional wrong word A range of trend and comparison words used accurately, with some less common choices (peak, plateau, double)
Grammatical Range and Accuracy Mostly simple sentences; complex ones contain errors; tense slips A mix of simple and complex sentences, many error-free; secure control of tenses and comparisons

A missing overview usually keeps Task Achievement at Band 5, however good the grammar is. That is why Module 13 teaches the overview in depth.

1.3 Question-type identification guide

Identify the visual first. It decides the vocabulary, the tenses and the paragraph plan.

Visual How to recognise it Typical wording Static or dynamic Main tenses Key skill
Line graph One or more lines, time along the bottom "shows changes in … between … and …" Dynamic Past simple for past dates Trends, peaks, crossings
Bar chart Vertical or horizontal bars "compares …", "shows …" Either Past or present simple Comparing bar heights; change if two dates
Pie chart A circle split into slices that total 100% "shows the proportion of …" Static, or two pies for change Present or past simple Largest and smallest shares; change between pies
Table Rows and columns of figures "gives information about …" Either Depends on the dates Selecting and grouping figures
Mixed charts Two different visuals on one topic "The charts show …" Either Depends on each visual Linking two sets of data
Process Steps joined by arrows; natural or manufactured "shows how … is produced" Not applicable Present simple passive Sequence and stages
Map Two or more maps of one place, or a plan for a future one "show changes to …", "proposed development" Past to present, or present to future Past simple or future forms Location, change, what stays the same
Floor plan The layout of a building or room "show the layout before and after …" Usually before and after Past and present Position and change
Life cycle Stages arranged in a circle "shows the life cycle of …" Not applicable Present simple Stages in order; the cycle closes
Flow chart Boxes, decisions and arrows "shows how a decision is made …" Not applicable Present simple passive Sequence and branches

1.4 Static or dynamic?

The first question for every visual is whether it shows change over time.

Dynamic Static
What you see Two or more dates One moment
What you describe Changes: rises, falls, peaks, stability Comparisons: highest, lowest, similar, ranking
The overview says The direction of the main changes, and which item ends highest or lowest Which items are largest and smallest, and how the groups differ
Tense Past simple for past dates; future forms for predictions Present simple; past simple if the date is in the past

Let the dates decide. Past dates take the past simple. No date, or the present, takes the present simple. Future dates take will, is expected to or is projected to. A mixed task may combine both, for example a 2024 table (static) with a line graph from 2000 to 2024 (dynamic): describe each by its type, then link them in the overview.

1.5 The five-look method (3 minutes)

  1. Labels. What is measured, in what unit (%, millions, hours, dollars), for whom and where?
  2. Time. One date or several? Past, present or future?
  3. Extremes. What are the highest and lowest values, and when do they happen?
  4. Patterns. What is the overall direction of each line or category: rising, falling, stable, or up and down?
  5. Groups. Which items move together, have similar values or change position? These groups become your paragraphs.

Mark the visual with a pencil as you look: circle the extremes, underline the unit, draw an arrow for each trend. Everything you mark is a candidate for the overview or a body paragraph.

1.6 The four-paragraph structure

Paragraph Job Length Key language Leave out
Introduction Say what the visual shows, in your own words 1–2 sentences, 25–35 words A different verb and noun from the task: compares, illustrates, proportion Copying the task; figures
Overview State the two or three biggest features 2–3 sentences, 40–55 words Overall, the most striking feature is that, whereas Detailed figures; reasons
Body 1 Present the first group of key details with data and comparisons 55–75 words Figures, comparatives, trend verbs Listing every number
Body 2 Present the remaining key details 55–75 words By contrast, whereas, respectively New topics; opinions

Why this order:

  • The overview comes second because the examiner looks for it early, and it makes every later detail easier to follow.
  • There is no conclusion. A conclusion only repeats the overview. Use the spare minute to check figures, or to add a comparison.
  • Two body paragraphs, grouped by idea, give the Coherence and Cohesion examiner a clear logic to follow. A paragraph per data series, or one long paragraph, hides it.

1.7 Choosing the data

Include Leave out
The highest and lowest values Every number on the visual
The biggest rise and the biggest fall Small ups and downs that do not change the picture
Start and end points of a trend Reasons ("because people prefer …")
Peaks, turning points and crossings Opinions and predictions
The exception: the one item that behaves differently Anything that is not on the visual

Group, do not list. Compare two ways of describing the same pie chart:

  • Weak: "Coal was 45%. Gas was 25%. Nuclear was 15%. Hydro was 10%. Wind and solar was 5%."
  • Strong: "Coal supplied 45% of electricity, nearly twice as much as gas (25%), while nuclear and hydroelectric power together provided 25%."

The strong version selects, compares and groups. Aim for about five to seven pieces of data in each body paragraph.

1.8 The universal 20-minute plan

Minutes Do Result
0–3 Five looks A marked-up visual
3–5 Group the features: decide the two body paragraphs and the overview points A two-line plan
5–8 Write the introduction and the overview About 70–90 words
8–17 Write the two body paragraphs About 100–130 words
17–20 Check figures, units, tenses, plurals and word count The final answer

1.9 Language builders, not templates

Choose a pattern because it fits what you see, never because you memorised it. Six builders cover most Task 1 sentences.

Builder Example
Item + verb + from A to B "Coffee consumption rose from 4 to 7 kilograms."
There was a(n) + adjective + noun + in + item "There was a sharp fall in unemployment."
Item + verb + adverb "Prices increased steadily."
A + was + comparative + than B "Rail travel was more popular than bus travel."
A, followed by B and C "Tea was the most popular drink, followed by coffee and juice."
Clause 1, whereas clause 2 "Sales of tablets doubled, whereas sales of laptops fell."

Modules 10 and 11 develop these into full vocabulary and grammar banks.

1.10 Check yourself before Module 2

  1. What is the difference between an overview and a conclusion?
  2. How can you tell if a visual is static or dynamic?
  3. Which tense do you use for a graph that ends in 2020? For a town map with no date? For a graph with predictions to 2035?
  4. Which three things would you mark on any chart?

Answers. (1) An overview comes near the start, names the main features and uses almost no figures; a conclusion repeats the ending and is not needed in Task 1. (2) Static shows one moment; dynamic shows two or more dates. (3) Past simple; present simple; future forms such as is expected to. (4) The highest and lowest values, the biggest change, and the exception or crossing.
