Module 2: Line graphs

A line graph shows how quantities change over time, so a Band 7 report describes the direction, size and timing of each change and then compares the lines.

  • B. How to analyse a line graph
  • C. Writing structure for a line graph

A. Understanding the question

What it shows. Time runs along the horizontal axis (days, months, years) and a quantity (a percentage, a number, a price) runs up the vertical axis. Each line is one item, such as a city, a product or an age group.

How to recognise it. One or more lines joined by points, with dates along the bottom. Lines mean the task is dynamic: the examiner wants trends.

What to look for. The unit and the period; the number of lines; where each line starts and ends; the highest and lowest points; turning points; and places where lines cross.

What the task asks. Select and report the main features, and make comparisons where relevant. It does not ask you to explain why the lines move.

Common wordings.

Wording What it means for your answer
“The graph shows changes in … between 2000 and 2020.” Past simple throughout
“The line graph illustrates the number of … from 1990 to 2015.” Past simple; paraphrase with the number of
“The graph gives information about … over a ten-year period.” Restate the period in your own words
“The graph shows … and predicts how they will change until 2040.” Past simple for past years; is expected to or will for the future
“The graph shows the percentage of … in three countries.” Compare the countries; group the lines

B. How to analyse a line graph

  1. Read the labels. Write down the unit (here, the percentage of commuters) and the period (2000–2020).
  2. Describe each line in three words. For example rose steadily, peaked then fell, fell then levelled off. These phrases become your overview.
  3. Note the starts and ends. Who is highest at the beginning? Who is highest at the end? A change in ranking is a major feature.
  4. Find turning points and crossings. A peak or a low point shows a change of direction; a crossing shows one line overtaking another.
  5. Group the lines. Lines with similar patterns, or the one line that is the exception, make natural paragraphs.
  6. Choose five to seven figures for each body paragraph: starting values, final values, peaks and low points.

Judging size. Compare each change with the scale. On a 0–30% axis a rise of 20 points is dramatic and a change of 1 point is negligible. Use slight or marginal for changes of roughly a tenth of the scale or less, and sharp or significant for changes of a third of the scale or more.

Pattern Describe it with
Steady rise rose steadily, a gradual increase
Sudden rise rose sharply, jumped, surged
Rise then fall peaked at … before falling
Fall then recovery dropped to a low of … before recovering
Little change remained stable, stayed at, levelled off
Up and down fluctuated between … and …

Include the start and end values, the highest and lowest points, peaks, crossings and the biggest change. Leave out every middle value, small wobbles, reasons (“because people bought bicycles”) and any figure that is not on the graph.

C. Writing structure for a line graph

Paragraph What to write Why
Introduction Paraphrase the task: what is measured, where, and the period Shows you understand the visual; no figures
Overview The two or three biggest trends, and any change in ranking between the start and the end Gives the examiner the big picture before the details
Body 1 The line with the clearest story, and the crossings it causes, with start, end and turning-point figures Groups related information instead of listing it
Body 2 The other lines, contrasted with Body 1 Completes the comparison; every paragraph keeps one purpose

Two ways to group: by line (best for three lines or fewer) or by time period, first half then second half, when many lines cross. Do not write one paragraph per line unless there are only two lines.

D. Band 7+ model answer

The line graph below shows the percentage of commuters who cycled to work in three cities between 2000 and 2020.

Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.

Write at least 150 words. (About 20 minutes)

Model answer · 178 words

The line graph compares the proportion of commuters who cycled to work in three cities over a twenty-year period, from 2000 to 2020.

Overall, cycling became far more popular in Northbury, whereas it declined in Southport, and Eastfield ended close to where it began after an initial rise. As a result, the city with the lowest figure in 2000 had the highest by 2020.

In 2000, Eastfield had the highest percentage of cyclists, at 20%, followed by Southport (14%) and Northbury (8%). Northbury then rose steadily, and its figure more than tripled to reach 28% in 2020, which made it the leading city. It overtook Southport between 2005 and 2010, and it passed Eastfield between 2015 and 2020.

The other two cities followed different patterns. Eastfield's figure climbed to a peak of 27% in 2010 before falling back to 21%, only one percentage point above its starting level. Southport, meanwhile, dropped from 14% to 9% by 2010, remained at that level in 2015, and then edged up to 10% in 2020, the lowest figure of the three cities.

E. The model answer explained

Paragraph 1: introduction

Sentence Why it works
“The line graph compares the proportion of commuters who cycled to work in three cities over a twenty-year period, from 2000 to 2020.” Compares replaces shows, proportion replaces percentage, and a twenty-year period restates the dates in a different form. One sentence covers the unit, the subject, the places and the time, and it contains no figures.

Paragraph 2: overview

Sentence Why it works
“Overall, cycling became far more popular in Northbury, whereas it declined in Southport, and Eastfield ended close to where it began after an initial rise.” It gives the direction of all three lines without figures. Whereas contrasts the two clearest trends, and the third line is summarised in the last clause.
“As a result, the city with the lowest figure in 2000 had the highest by 2020.” This is the most important comparison: the change in ranking from start to end. It is worded carefully. Eastfield was first in 2000 and second in 2020, so the sentence does not claim that the order was reversed.

Paragraph 3: first body paragraph (the city that grew)

Sentence Why it works
“In 2000, Eastfield had the highest percentage of cyclists, at 20%, followed by Southport (14%) and Northbury (8%).” It sets all three starting positions in one ranking sentence, using followed by and brackets to save words.
“Northbury then rose steadily, and its figure more than tripled to reach 28% in 2020, which made it the leading city.” More than tripled compares the end with the start (8 to 28 is a rise of 3.5 times) and says more than two numbers can. Which made it is a relative clause that links the figure to its meaning.
“It overtook Southport between 2005 and 2010, and it passed Eastfield between 2015 and 2020.” Crossings are key features. The graph has a point only every five years, so between is accurate; naming one exact year would be a guess.

Paragraph 4: second body paragraph (the other two cities)

Sentence Why it works
“The other two cities followed different patterns.” A short topic sentence tells the reader what the paragraph covers, which helps coherence.
“Eastfield's figure climbed to a peak of 27% in 2010 before falling back to 21%, only one percentage point above its starting level.” A peak and a final value in one sentence. Before falling is a participle clause. Percentage point is the correct unit for the difference between two percentages.
“Southport, meanwhile, dropped from 14% to 9% by 2010, remained at that level in 2015, and then edged up to 10% in 2020, the lowest figure of the three cities.” Three verbs of different strength (dropped, remained, edged up) describe a fall, a plateau and a tiny rise. That level avoids repeating the number, and the final phrase completes the comparison.

How the answer meets the four criteria

Criterion Evidence in the answer
Task Achievement A clear overview with the main trends and the change in ranking; every key feature is covered (starting order, Northbury's growth, both crossings, Eastfield's peak, Southport's fall and slight recovery); the figures are accurate; there are no opinions.
Coherence and Cohesion One idea per paragraph; linking words (whereas, then, meanwhile, before, as a result); reference words (it, its, that level); the ranking sentence at the start of Body 1 leads naturally into the story.
Lexical Resource A range of trend verbs (rose, climbed, dropped, edged up, remained, overtook, passed); noun phrases (a peak of, starting level); paraphrase (proportion, twenty-year period); precise comparison (more than tripled, percentage point).
Grammatical Range and Accuracy Past simple controlled throughout; relative clauses (who cycled, which made it); a participle clause (before falling); comparatives and superlatives (far more popular, the highest, the lowest); complex sentences with whereas.
