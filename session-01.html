How the Pyramids Were Built

AThe Great Pyramid at Giza is the most famous, but it is only one of more than a hundred pyramids that survive in Egypt. How such enormous monuments were built more than 4,500 years ago, without modern machines, has puzzled visitors for centuries. Archaeologists now understand much of the process, although some questions remain open.
BThe stone blocks were cut in quarries and then moved to the building site. Much of the ordinary limestone came from quarries close by, but finer stone, including the granite used inside the Great Pyramid, was transported hundreds of kilometres, usually by boat along the River Nile. Over land, the blocks were dragged on wooden sledges, since wheels would simply have sunk into the soft sand.
CAn ancient wall painting shows a worker pouring water onto the sand in front of a sledge carrying a huge statue. For a long time, this was thought to be a religious ritual. Modern experiments, however, have shown that sand with the right amount of water becomes firmer, so that much less force is needed to pull a heavy load across it. Teams of workers hauled the sledges with ropes, and oxen may sometimes have helped.
DOnce the blocks reached the site, they were probably raised using ramps made of mud brick and rubble. Exactly how these ramps were arranged, whether straight, zigzagging or spiralling around the pyramid, is still debated by experts.
EA popular belief is that the pyramids were built by slaves. Excavations near Giza, however, have uncovered the villages where the builders lived and the cemeteries where they were buried close to the pyramids, with bread and beer for the afterlife. Such honours would hardly have been given to slaves, and most experts now believe the builders were organised teams of respected workers.
FNor were the pyramids perfected at the first attempt. The earliest, the Step Pyramid at Saqqara, designed by the architect Imhotep, consists of six stone platforms placed one on top of another. Under King Sneferu, builders tried to create smooth sides: one pyramid had to change its angle halfway up, giving it a bent shape, before the Red Pyramid finally succeeded. Many people still imagine that the pyramids were built with the help of mysterious forces. The real explanation is less magical but more impressive: skill, planning and generations of trial and error.

Learn from the passage: key vocabulary

These words often appear in IELTS passages. Click each card for the meaning and an example.

The two hardest answers are usually FALSE and NOT GIVEN. Ask: does the passage say the opposite (FALSE), or does it say nothing (NOT GIVEN)?
3

The three answers

T
TRUE: the passage says the same thing, usually in different words.Passage: “His father worked at a mill.” Statement: “His father was a miller.”
F
FALSE: the passage says the opposite.Passage: “His father worked at a mill.” Statement: “His father was an architect.”
NG
NOT GIVEN: the passage says nothing about it. You cannot say true or false.Passage: “His father worked at a mill.” Statement: “His father was rich.”
4

A four-step method

1
Underline keywords in the statement, especially names, numbers and dates that are easy to scan for.
2
Find the place in the passage. Answers follow the order of the passage.
3
Compare carefully. Check small words: only, all, never, always, first, most, some. One word can change the answer.
4
Decide. Same meaning: TRUE. Opposite meaning: FALSE. Missing information: NOT GIVEN.

Warm-up: spot the trap word

Passage sentence: “Teams of workers hauled the sledges, and oxen may sometimes have helped.”

      Model answers

      Open these only after you have written your own report. Compare the overview first: it matters most.

      Simpler model (about 165 words)

      The two pie charts show how people in Canada and Australia get their news from five different sources: TV, the internet, newspapers, radio and other sources.

      Overall, TV and online news are the most popular sources in both countries. However, TV is the main source in Canada, while online news is the main source in Australia.

      In Canada, TV is the most popular source of news, at 40%. Online news comes second with 36%. Newspapers account for 14%, which is twice the figure for radio (7%). The remaining 3% comes from other sources.

      In Australia, more than half of people (52%) use online news, which is much higher than in Canada. TV comes second at 37%, slightly lower than the Canadian figure. Only 7% read newspapers, half the Canadian figure, and just 2% listen to the radio. Another 2% use other sources.

      Higher-level model (about 185 words)

      The two pie charts compare the proportions of people in Canada and Australia who obtain their news from five different sources: television, online platforms, print media, radio and unspecified sources.

      Overall, television and online platforms are the dominant sources of news in both countries. However, while television is slightly more popular than online media in Canada, the opposite is true in Australia, where online platforms account for more than half of the total. Radio and print media play a minor role in both nations.

      In Canada, television is the leading source, accounting for 40% of the total, closely followed by online platforms at 36%. Print media represents 14%, twice the proportion who rely on radio (7%), while the remaining 3% comes from unspecified sources.

      In Australia, by contrast, online platforms dominate, at 52%, which is 16 percentage points higher than in Canada. Television ranks second at 37%, slightly below the Canadian figure. Print media accounts for just 7%, half the Canadian proportion, and radio and unspecified sources each make up only 2%.

      Notice that neither model ends with “In conclusion”. In Task 1, the overview does the job of a conclusion.

      Andrea Palladio: From Stonemason to Master Architect

      AAndrea Palladio, born in Padua in 1508, is often described as the most influential architect in Western history. His work inspired so many later buildings that a whole style, Palladian architecture, is named after him. Yet he came from a modest background: his father worked at a mill, grinding grain.
      BAt thirteen, the boy was apprenticed to a stonemason in Padua, and a few years later he moved to the nearby city of Vicenza. There he joined a workshop of stonecutters, carving decorative details such as columns, doorways and fireplaces. His skill with stone gave him a practical understanding of building that many architects of his time lacked.
      CHis life changed when he met Gian Giorgio Trissino, a wealthy scholar who recognised his talent. Trissino arranged his education in mathematics and classical literature, gave him the name “Palladio”, and took him to Rome, where he spent months measuring and drawing the ruins of ancient temples and other buildings.
      DBack in the Veneto region, Palladio designed country villas for landowners who wanted houses that were also working farms. Many of these villas have a portico at the front: a porch with columns, topped by a triangular roof called a pediment, an idea borrowed from ancient temples. The best known, the Villa Rotonda near Vicenza, has a portico on each of its four sides.
      EIn Venice, he designed two famous churches, San Giorgio Maggiore and Il Redentore. Both stand across the water from the historic centre of the city, so their white fronts can be admired from a distance.
      FPalladio’s influence spread mainly through a book. In 1570 he published “The Four Books of Architecture”, which explained his ideas with clear illustrations. Decades after Palladio’s death in 1580, the English architect Inigo Jones travelled to Italy, studied his buildings and drawings, and brought the style to England. In the eighteenth century, Thomas Jefferson, who later became president of the United States, used Palladian ideas when he designed his own house, Monticello.
      GSome critics point out that Palladio worked mostly for the rich and powerful. That may be true, but it hardly reduces his achievement. Five centuries later, the calm balance and clear proportions of his buildings still give visitors a feeling of peace, and in my view no architect has been more widely imitated.
