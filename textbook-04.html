Module 4: Pie charts and tables

Pie charts and tables both present shares and figures at a moment in time, so the Band 7 skill is the same: select the biggest, smallest and most unusual figures, group them, and compare them instead of reading them out one by one.

  • Part 1: Pie charts
  • Part 2: Tables

Part 1: Pie charts

A. Understanding the question

What it shows. A pie chart divides a whole (always 100%) into slices. Each slice is a share of that whole. Two or more pies are used to compare dates, places or groups.

How to recognise it. A circle cut into slices, with labels or a key, and percentages that add up to 100%.

What to look for. What the whole is (what does 100% stand for, and when?); the categories; the largest and smallest slices; slices of similar size; and, when there are several pies, which slices grew, shrank or stayed the same.

What the task asks. Select and report the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.

Common wordings.

Wording What it means for your answer
“The pie chart shows the proportion of …” One pie, one moment: rank and compare the slices
“The pie charts show … in 2000 and 2020.” Two pies: compare each slice and describe change
“The charts show the percentage of … in three countries.” Compare countries; group the similar ones
“The pie charts show the results of a survey of …” Past simple for the survey; say respondents or people surveyed

A warning about shares. A pie shows shares, not amounts. If the total changes between two pies, a larger share does not always mean a larger amount. Write about shares, proportions or percentages unless the task gives amounts.

Useful language for shares. nearly half (45–49%), just under a quarter (23–24%), a third, the majority (over 50%), a small minority, one in five (20%), twice as much as.

B. How to analyse a pie chart

  1. Find the whole. What does the full circle stand for, and for which year or place?
  2. Rank the slices in each pie, from largest to smallest.
  3. Sort them by size. Large (a quarter or more), medium, and small slices. The extremes will form your overview.
  4. Compare the pies. Work out each slice's change in percentage points, and note any change in the ranking.
  5. Turn numbers into fractions and ratios. 45% is nearly half; 45% compared with 25% is nearly twice as much; 5% to 27% is more than fivefold.
  6. Select the largest, the smallest, the biggest change, and the slices that stayed stable.

A worked change analysis:

Source Change (percentage points) How to say it
Coal −27 fell sharply; no longer the largest source
Gas +7 rose moderately; became the largest source
Nuclear −1 barely changed
Hydroelectric −1 barely changed
Wind and solar +22 increased more than fivefold; the biggest rise

The changes add up to zero, as they must when both pies total 100%. Use that as a check on your arithmetic.

C. Writing structure for pie charts

Paragraph What to write Why
Introduction Paraphrase the task: what the whole is, where, and the dates Shows you understand the visual
Overview The largest slice in each pie, and the biggest changes between the pies The big picture, with no detail
Body 1 The first pie (or the largest slices), with the leading slices compared Sets the baseline using ranking and ratios
Body 2 The second pie, with each change measured against the first Compares instead of repeating the first paragraph

Do not describe the first pie in full and then the second in full. Compare each slice across the pies as soon as you mention it.

D. Band 7+ model answer

The pie charts below show the sources of electricity generated in one country in 2000 and 2020.

Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.

Write at least 150 words. (About 20 minutes)

Model answer · 162 words

The two pie charts compare how electricity was generated from five different sources in one country in 2000 and 2020.

Overall, the country relied much less on coal and much more on gas and on wind and solar power by 2020. Coal was by far the largest source in 2000, but gas had taken its place at the top by 2020, while nuclear and hydroelectric power changed very little.

In 2000, coal accounted for 45% of the country's electricity, which was nearly twice as much as gas, the second-largest source, at 25%. Nuclear power supplied 15% and hydroelectric power 10%, while wind and solar power contributed only 5%.

By 2020, the picture had changed considerably. The share of coal fell sharply to 18%, whereas gas rose to 32% and became the leading source. The biggest rise was in wind and solar power, which increased more than fivefold to 27%. By contrast, nuclear and hydroelectric power barely changed, at 14% and 9% respectively.

E. The model answer explained

Paragraph 1: introduction

Sentence Why it works
“The two pie charts compare how electricity was generated from five different sources in one country in 2000 and 2020.” The task said show and sources of electricity generated; the answer uses compare and how electricity was generated from, a change of verb and structure. It names the whole (electricity), the number of categories and the two dates.

Paragraph 2: overview

Sentence Why it works
“Overall, the country relied much less on coal and much more on gas and on wind and solar power by 2020.” One sentence states the main movement, away from coal and towards two other sources, with no figures. Relied turns percentages into a meaningful idea.
“Coal was by far the largest source in 2000, but gas had taken its place at the top by 2020, while nuclear and hydroelectric power changed very little.” It gives the change in the leading slice and the stable slices. Together with the first sentence, this covers all five categories without a single number.

Paragraph 3: the first pie

Sentence Why it works
“In 2000, coal accounted for 45% of the country's electricity, which was nearly twice as much as gas, the second-largest source, at 25%.” Accounted for is the standard verb for a share. The relative clause which was nearly twice as much as gas turns two numbers into a comparison (45 ÷ 25 = 1.8), and the second-largest source gives rank at the same time.
“Nuclear power supplied 15% and hydroelectric power 10%, while wind and solar power contributed only 5%.” The three smaller slices go in one sentence. Supplied and contributed avoid repeating accounted for, and only marks the smallest slice.

Paragraph 4: the second pie and the changes

Sentence Why it works
“By 2020, the picture had changed considerably.” A short link sentence signals the change of date. The past perfect shows that the change was complete by 2020.
“The share of coal fell sharply to 18%, whereas gas rose to 32% and became the leading source.” Each slice is measured against 2000 as soon as it is mentioned, so the reader never has to look back. Whereas contrasts the two biggest movements.
“The biggest rise was in wind and solar power, which increased more than fivefold to 27%.” More than fivefold is exact (27 ÷ 5 = 5.4) and more informative than “from 5% to 27%” alone. The percentage-point change (+22) is the largest increase, which is why this is called the biggest rise.
“By contrast, nuclear and hydroelectric power barely changed, at 14% and 9% respectively.” The stable slices close the paragraph, and respectively pairs two figures with two names.

How the answer meets the four criteria

Criterion Evidence in the answer
Task Achievement A clear overview covering all five sources; the largest and smallest slices and the biggest changes are selected; figures are accurate and checked (the changes sum to zero); no opinions or reasons.
Coherence and Cohesion One idea per paragraph; the link sentence By 2020, the picture had changed considerably; whereas, by contrast, respectively and which connect ideas inside sentences.
Lexical Resource accounted for, supplied, contributed, relied on, fivefold, taken its place, barely changed; verbs of share are varied.
Grammatical Range and Accuracy Past simple and past perfect; relative clauses (which was nearly twice as much as gas, which increased); comparatives (much less, much more); whereas clauses; accurate use of the share of and the second-largest.

Part 2: Tables

A. Understanding the question

What it shows. A table holds many figures in rows and columns. The row headings name one set of items, the column headings name another, and each cell is a figure. Nothing is drawn for you, so you must find the pattern yourself.

How to recognise it. A grid with headings. It may show one date or several, percentages or amounts, and sometimes totals.

What to look for. The unit; what the rows and columns stand for; the highest and lowest figure in each row and each column; patterns across a row or down a column; exceptions; and totals, if given.

What the task asks. Select and report the main features, and make comparisons where relevant. A table invites you to describe everything, which is the most common mistake.

Common wordings.

Wording What it means for your answer
“The table gives information about …” Paraphrase with shows, compares or provides data on
“The table below shows the percentage of people who …” Compare groups with percentage points and ratios
“The table shows … in 2000, 2010 and 2020.” Several dates: describe trends across the columns
“The table shows the number of … in six countries.” Rank the countries; group the high and low ones

B. How to analyse a table

  1. Read the headings and the unit. What do the rows and columns stand for? Are the figures percentages, amounts or averages?
  2. Choose the direction. Read across the rows (one service in every age group) or down the columns (one age group's profile). Use whichever shows the clearer pattern, and base your paragraphs on it.
  3. Mark the extremes. In each row, circle the highest and lowest figure.
  4. Find the pattern. Do the figures rise or fall steadily across the columns? Is there a peak in the middle?
  5. Spot the exception. One row or column that does not follow the pattern is often a key feature.
  6. Group. Put rows or columns with the same pattern together.
  7. Select about 8 to 12 figures for the whole answer; leave out the rest.

The table used in this module is below. Read it first, then compare your own analysis with the one that follows.

Service 16–24 25–44 45–64 65+
Email 88 96 92 71
Social media 97 85 58 29
Online banking 62 89 78 41
Online shopping 74 91 77 38
Video streaming 95 82 49 22

A worked pattern analysis (reading across the rows):

Service Highest group Lowest group Pattern
Email 25–44 65+ A majority of every group uses it
Social media 16–24 65+ Falls with every older group; a 68-point gap
Online banking 25–44 65+ Peaks in the middle, then falls
Online shopping 25–44 65+ Peaks in the middle, then falls
Video streaming 16–24 65+ Falls steadily with age

Two findings stand out: the 65+ group has the lowest figure for every service, and the services split into two types (young-led and middle-led). Those become the overview.

C. Writing structure for tables

Paragraph What to write Why
Introduction Paraphrase the task: what the figures measure, for whom, and when Shows you understand the table
Overview The one or two clearest patterns across the whole table, with no figures The big picture the table does not show by itself
Body 1 The first group of rows or columns, with the extreme figures compared Selects and groups; avoids reading out row after row
Body 2 The remaining group, linked back to the first Completes the comparison and shows the trend

The most common table mistake is describing it row by row. Always group by pattern: here, the younger groups first, then the older ones.

D. Band 7+ model answer

The table below shows the percentage of people in four age groups who used five online services in 2024.

Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.

Write at least 150 words. (About 20 minutes)

The table is shown in section B above. (In the exam it appears with the task. Figures are percentages of each age group.)

Model answer · 172 words

The table compares how many people in four age groups used five different online services in 2024.

Overall, age had a strong influence on internet use. Younger people were the most likely to use social media and video streaming, whereas people aged 25 to 44 made the most use of online banking and shopping. Email was the only service used by a clear majority of every age group.

Looking at the youngest group first, 97% of 16- to 24-year-olds used social media and 95% streamed video, compared with just 62% who used online banking. The 25 to 44 age group had the highest figures for email (96%), online banking (89%) and online shopping (91%), and their use of social media (85%) and streaming (82%) was also high.

After the age of 44, the figures for every service declined, particularly among people over 65. Only 29% of this group used social media and 22% watched streamed video, the two lowest figures in the table. Even so, email remained popular with 71% of over-65s.

E. The model answer explained

Paragraph 1: introduction

Sentence Why it works
“The table compares how many people in four age groups used five different online services in 2024.” Compares how many people replaces shows the percentage of people, which changes both the verb and the structure. It states the groups, the number of services and the year, and it uses no figures.

Paragraph 2: overview

Sentence Why it works
“Overall, age had a strong influence on internet use.” The single biggest idea in the table, in plain words. It is a summary of the data and not an opinion.
“Younger people were the most likely to use social media and video streaming, whereas people aged 25 to 44 made the most use of online banking and shopping.” It names the two types of service and which age group leads each, with no numbers. Whereas shows the contrast.
“Email was the only service used by a clear majority of every age group.” The exception, and a check that it is true: only email stays above 70% in every group. Noticing exceptions is a mark of a high-band answer.

Paragraph 3: the younger groups

Sentence Why it works
“Looking at the youngest group first, 97% of 16- to 24-year-olds used social media and 95% streamed video, compared with just 62% who used online banking.” A short opening phrase orients the reader. The two highest figures for this group are contrasted with its lowest one, so the numbers support a comparison. Compared with just adds a judgement of size.
“The 25 to 44 age group had the highest figures for email (96%), online banking (89%) and online shopping (91%), and their use of social media (85%) and streaming (82%) was also high.” Three leading figures are grouped in one list with brackets. The second clause shows the group's other strengths, so no row is described alone.

Paragraph 4: the older groups

Sentence Why it works
“After the age of 44, the figures for every service declined, particularly among people over 65.” One sentence gives the pattern for the two older groups. The claim is true for all five rows, because every 45–64 figure is below its 25–44 figure and every 65+ figure is below its 45–64 figure.
“Only 29% of this group used social media and 22% watched streamed video, the two lowest figures in the table.” The extremes of the whole table. This group avoids repeating people over 65, and the final noun phrase is a short apposition.
“Even so, email remained popular with 71% of over-65s.” Even so returns to the exception named in the overview, which ties the answer together.

How the answer meets the four criteria

Criterion Evidence in the answer
Task Achievement A clear overview that names the pattern and the exception; only the extreme figures are selected (11 of the 20 figures in the table); all figures are accurate; no opinions.
Coherence and Cohesion Paragraphs by age (the younger groups, then the older ones) and not by service; whereas, even so, compared with, after the age of 44; this group and their as reference words.
Lexical Resource made the most use of, a clear majority, streamed, declined, the two lowest figures, remained popular; the same item (use) is expressed in several ways.
Grammatical Range and Accuracy Past simple throughout; comparatives and superlatives (the most likely, the highest, the two lowest); a participial phrase (looking at the youngest group first); relative and reduced clauses; accurate use of percent figures with of.
